# Critical Evaluation of an Exposure-Coupled Flood Decision-Support Tool for Humanitarian NGOs in South Sudan

## Introduction

The Republic of South Sudan represents one of the most complex, protracted humanitarian crises globally, characterized by compounding shocks of sub-national violence, macroeconomic instability, and increasingly severe climatic extremes. Since 2019, the country has experienced consecutive years of unprecedented, record-breaking flooding, fundamentally altering the physical and demographic landscape of states such as Unity, Jonglei, and Upper Nile. This continuous state of inundation has precipitated the displacement of millions, decimated agricultural livelihoods, and exacerbated a severe acute food insecurity crisis that currently afflicts more than half of the national population. In this exceptionally challenging operating environment, the efficient allocation of highly constrained humanitarian resources is a paramount concern for international non-governmental organizations (INGOs).

A proposed student research project seeks to address this challenge by developing a county-level, exposure-coupled flood decision-support tool. The proposed architectural framework of this tool intends to integrate flood hazard estimates—theoretically derived from satellite-observed inundation persistence, upstream lake levels, and seasonal rainfall outlooks—with disaggregated exposure layers. These exposure layers ostensibly include WorldPop demographic data, the Anomaly Hotspots of Agricultural Production (ASAP) cropland layers, and the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) livelihood zones. The stated objective of this geospatial integration is to furnish NGOs, particularly consortiums such as the Dutch Relief Alliance (DRA), with a dynamically ranked list of priority counties. This ranking is intended to optimize the geographic targeting and pre-positioning of aid during the May–June dry season, theoretically allowing organizations to circumvent the exorbitant costs associated with wet-season air transport.

While the conceptual design of this decision-support tool is intuitively appealing from a geospatial engineering perspective, its practical utility within the highly constrained, deeply politicized operational reality of South Sudan requires rigorous and objective scrutiny. Technocratic solutions to humanitarian targeting frequently risk assuming an idealized operational environment, abstracting away the political economy of aid, the structural rigidity of consortium funding, the physical hydrodynamics of equatorial river systems, and the severe epistemological limits of earth observation data in conflict zones. This report comprehensively evaluates the core assumptions underpinning the proposed tool. By systematically examining the redundancy of the product against existing humanitarian mechanisms, the empirical realities of NGO decision-making, the hydrodynamic predictability of the Sudd wetland, the validity of the proposed evaluation design, the logistics premise, and underlying data quality constraints, this analysis determines whether the proposed tool offers genuine operational added value or merely duplicates existing systems in a manner incongruous with the realities of aid delivery in South Sudan.

## Redundancy with Existing Humanitarian Systems

The foundational premise of the proposed tool is that a critical deficit exists in the availability of county-level, exposure-coupled flood risk prioritization products tailored for humanitarian actors in South Sudan. An objective, comprehensive appraisal of the current humanitarian information management ecosystem in the region invalidates this assumption entirely. The landscape is already heavily saturated with highly sophisticated, operationally integrated analytical products that execute the exact functions proposed by the student project, often with access to superior primary data networks and institutional integration.

The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) publishes the annual Humanitarian Needs Overview (HNO), which aggregates multi-sectoral vulnerabilities to produce a definitive severity ranking and a precise People in Need (PiN) estimate for every county in South Sudan. The HNO explicitly incorporates the impacts of extraordinary flooding, localized drought, and inter-communal conflict into its inter-sectoral analysis. For example, the HNO systematically ranks counties along a severity spectrum, identifying locales such as Panyikang as facing catastrophic need, while simultaneously projecting the varying degrees of extreme and severe need across the remaining 78 counties. Similarly, the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) framework provides regular, sub-national projections of acute food insecurity, synthesizing extensive primary data on crop loss, livestock mortality, and disrupted livelihoods directly resulting from climatic shocks. The IPC effectively translates flood exposure into actionable humanitarian intelligence regarding household vulnerability, negating the need for an external tool to infer livelihood impacts from raw flood masks.

Furthermore, the intersection of flood hazard masks with disaggregated exposure layers (population, cropland, and livestock) is already a routine, highly automated operational output of several specialized agencies. The United Nations Satellite Centre (UNOSAT) and the Copernicus Emergency Management Service routinely publish satellite-detected surface water extents, explicitly intersecting these hazard layers with WorldPop demographic data and localized cropland estimates to produce quantifiable sub-national exposure metrics. UNOSAT’s flood impact analyses for South Sudan regularly delineate the precise acreage of inundated cropland and the number of potentially affected populations at both the state and county levels. For instance, recent operational outputs have precisely quantified agricultural exposure, noting when an estimated 16.5 thousand hectares of cropland are potentially affected by rising waters in specific administrative zones.

The REACH Initiative and IMPACT Initiatives operate the Climate Watch system, frequently disseminating comprehensive flood hazard and susceptibility assessments for South Sudan and the broader region. These products incorporate highly granular multifactorial criteria, including elevation, slope, topographic wetness indices, soil type, and land cover, to identify populations and agricultural zones at high risk of displacement. At the regional forecasting level, the IGAD Climate Prediction and Applications Centre (ICPAC) and the Flood-PROOFS East Africa system maintain regional flood risk profiles that actively forecast and monitor inundation anomalies across the Greater Horn of Africa. Additionally, the Food and Agriculture Organization's Data in Emergencies Hub (FAO DIEM) and the Global Information and Early Warning System (GIEWS) generate high-frequency, household-level surveys combined with geospatial analysis to continuously monitor cropland and livestock exposure to flooding. Recent FAO DIEM assessments have successfully quantified over 1.7 million hectares of affected cropland across the region, demonstrating a level of operational monitoring that a student-derived ASAP cropland intersection could not meaningfully improve upon.

The evidence overwhelmingly indicates that the generation of exposure-coupled flood maps is not a novel contribution, nor is it the limiting factor in the humanitarian response. Comprehensive evaluations of humanitarian action in South Sudan—including the ALNAP State of the Humanitarian System report—consistently highlight that the actual operational constraints for NGOs are not analytical deficits, but rather an extreme scarcity of funding, systemic bureaucratic access impediments, severe logistical bottlenecks, and targeted violence against aid workers. The humanitarian sector is currently navigating a generational funding collapse, with coordinated appeals vastly underfunded, compelling organizations to hyper-prioritize assistance based on available financial resources and physical access rather than theoretical risk rankings.

Furthermore, government forces and non-state armed groups in South Sudan frequently deny access to specific populations as a deliberate, weaponized tactic of war. This reality renders hazard-based prioritization entirely subordinate to political and security dynamics. Therefore, a new exposure-coupled tool would merely duplicate an already crowded marketplace of information products while systematically failing to address the true structural impediments that govern aid delivery.

## The Reality of NGO Geographic Targeting and Decision-Making

The proposed tool operates on the mechanistic assumption that INGOs such as ZOA, Dorcas, Help a Child, and other members of the Dutch Relief Alliance (DRA) possess the institutional agility to dynamically alter their geographic footprint on an annual basis in response to seasonal flood forecasts. However, a rigorous examination of the political economy of aid consortiums and the structural realities of INGO programming in South Sudan reveals that this assumption is fundamentally detached from operational practice.

INGOs in South Sudan do not select their geographic target areas through a detached, annual algorithmic ranking of environmental risk. Rather, geographic targeting is deeply constrained by pre-existing field presence, the location of established logistical bases, long-term donor earmarking, cluster assignments, and complex, formalized partnership agreements. The Dutch Relief Alliance's South Sudan Joint Response (SSJR) perfectly exemplifies this structural rigidity. The SSJR is executed in multi-year strategic cycles, most recently encompassing the 2018–2021, 2022–2023, and 2024–2026 periods. The geographic footprints for these responses are negotiated and fixed at the inception of the multi-year funding cycle, locking partners into specific operational theatres for the duration of the grant.

For instance, the SSJR 2024–2026 framework explicitly outlines continued operations in specific, pre-determined locales, including Malakal, Melut, Tonj North, and the Pibor Administrative Area, while formally initiating highly planned, multi-year activities in the Abyei Administrative Area, Akobo County, Mayendit County, and Rubkona County. These county portfolios are not subject to dynamic annual reshuffling. The rigidity of this geographic footprint is inextricably linked to the localization agenda, which is a central strategic objective of the DRA and the broader Grand Bargain commitments. INGOs such as ZOA, Save the Children, and Help a Child operate by forming deep, multi-year capacity-building partnerships with local and national NGOs—such as the Charity Empowerment Foundation (CEF), Smile Again Africa Development Organization (SAADO), and the Women Development Group (WDG).

These local partners are deeply embedded within specific communities and possess the nuanced relational networks required to negotiate access with local authorities and armed factions—a necessity in a landscape characterized by fragmented authority and hybrid forms of collaboration. An INGO cannot simply abandon a local partner in Tonj North and rapidly establish a new operational base in Aweil South merely because a seasonal forecast model ranks the latter slightly higher in immediate flood risk for the upcoming quarter. Doing so would violate legally binding sub-grant agreements, destroy localized trust, undermine the overarching mandate to shift power and resources to national actors, and ignore the immense logistical and bureaucratic costs of initiating operations in a new county.

Furthermore, documented evidence of NGOs changing their geographic targeting year-to-year in response to dynamic hazard data is virtually nonexistent in South Sudan. When geographic shifts do occur, they are almost exclusively driven by acute conflict dynamics that render an existing operational area entirely inaccessible. For example, during a previous SSJR cycle, Dorcas was forced to abandon its planned intervention in Wau South and relocate to Wau North. This decision was not motivated by climatic variables or flood mapping, but rather because intense fighting erupted between government forces and the SPLA-IO, transforming the original location into a restricted military zone. Consequently, an analytical tool designed to trigger dynamic, annual re-ranking of counties is structurally incompatible with the multi-year funding cycles, localization mandates, and conflict-driven access realities that entirely govern INGO decision-making in South Sudan.

## Predictability, Skill, and Hydrodynamics of the Sudd Hazard Input

The student project hypothesizes that upstream lake levels, prior-season inundation persistence, and seasonal rainfall outlooks (such as those from ICPAC or ECMWF at 2-3 month lead times) can demonstrate verified predictive skill for county-level flood extent in the Sudd wetland. While there is profound predictability within the White Nile system, it does not operate on the rapid temporal scale assumed by standard seasonal rainfall outlooks, rendering short-term meteorological forecasting largely irrelevant for regional inundation dynamics.

Recent, authoritative hydrological research on the Victoria–Kyoga–Albert–Sudd cascade has fundamentally rewritten the scientific understanding of flood propagation in this basin. Historically, based on the basic alignment of seasonal rainfall peaks and downstream flooding, it was widely assumed in foundational texts—such as Sutcliffe and Parks' (1999) *The Hydrology of the Nile*—that a flood wave from Lake Victoria required approximately four to five months to reach the Sudd wetland. This assumption heavily influenced earlier hydrological models, which treated the Sudd as a relatively responsive reservoir.

However, extensive modern flood-wave tracking published in the journal *Hydrology and Earth System Sciences* (HESS) in 2026, utilizing daily lake levels, discharge data, and MODIS-derived inundation from 2002 to 2024, demonstrates a mean system transit time of 16.84 months (range 13.0–20.9 months)—substantially longer than the commonly inferred timescale. Segmental analysis of this complex lake-river-wetland corridor indicates rapid transmission from Lake Victoria to Lake Kyoga (mean 4.2 months), followed by severe hydrodynamic attenuation through the Albert-Sudd reach (mean 9.3 months). This extreme attenuation is dictated by extensive floodplain storage, backwater control, and the massive biological buffering capacity of the wetland.

This 17-month transit time implies that flood exposure in South Sudanese counties (e.g., Unity, Jonglei, Upper Nile) is not primarily a function of concurrent or 2-3 month seasonal rainfall, but rather the delayed manifestation of multi-year upstream storage anomalies. For instance, the unprecedented, catastrophic flooding in South Sudan between 2020 and 2024 was initiated by a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) anomaly in late 2019, which raised Lake Victoria to record levels, peaking at an exceptional 1136.66 m a.s.l. in 2024 (exceeding the historical 1964 maximum). The resulting flood wave continuously saturated the Sudd, pushing it past its historical maximum extents year over year, reaching an annual maximum of 163,475 km² in 2022.

Once the Sudd is saturated, its immense storage capacity and slow drainage dictate that it can sustain severe flooding over multiple years, even under suppressed local rainfall conditions, exactly as was observed during the rainfall deficits of 2022. Therefore, using 2-3 month seasonal rainfall forecasts to predict county-level flooding in the Sudd is hydrologically flawed. The system is characterized by "long memory," where upstream storage governs downstream flood risk over multi-season timescales.

Furthermore, because the inter-annual variation in flood exposure is dictated by this slow-moving, multi-year saturation, the spatial distribution of the flood hazard is highly persistent. Counties inundated in one year are exceedingly likely to remain inundated the following year. Consequently, the practical value of a dynamic predictive model collapses; a static map of chronically flooded counties performs equivalently to a complex predictive algorithm, as the exact same 10-15 counties (e.g., Rubkona, Ayod, Pibor, Fangak, Mayendit) endure the vast majority of the exposure year after year. The student project's assertion that inter-annual variation is volatile enough to necessitate dynamic annual re-ranking is contradicted by the fundamental hydrodynamics of the "Mega-lake Sudd" system.

## Epistemological Limits of Evaluation Design and Ground Truth

The proposed evaluation design intends to use satellite-derived exposure—specifically WorldPop demographic data intersected with optical flood masks—as the ground truth to validate the predictive tool. This methodology introduces severe epistemological flaws, primarily driven by circular reasoning and the pervasive reporting biases inherent to humanitarian data collection in active conflict zones.

Validating a satellite-derived hazard predictor using a satellite-derived exposure metric as ground truth is fundamentally a tautological exercise. If both the input model and the evaluation metric rely on similar earth observation parameters, the validation process merely confirms the internal consistency and autocorrelation of the satellite algorithms rather than their accuracy in reflecting physical, on-the-ground reality. To achieve genuinely independent validation, the tool would need to be tested against independent, multi-year observed flood impact datasets, such as the International Organization for Migration's (IOM) Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), OCHA affected figures, or REACH ground assessments.

However, utilizing these humanitarian datasets for retrospective skill evaluation introduces a fatal observational bias. In South Sudan, humanitarian data collection is strictly bounded by bureaucratic access constraints, physical isolation, and active hostilities. Government forces and non-state armed actors (such as the SPLA-IO and the National Salvation Front) frequently utilize access denial as a strategic weapon, systematically preventing NGOs and UN agencies from conducting assessments in opposition-held or contested territories (as frequently observed in the Equatoria regions and parts of Upper Nile). Consequently, DTM displacement figures, OCHA affected population numbers, and REACH assessment coverages are not comprehensive representations of where floods actually occurred; rather, they are geographical maps of where humanitarians were politically and physically permitted to travel safely.

If a flood prediction model correctly identifies severe inundation in a highly contested, inaccessible county, but the DTM registers zero displaced persons in that county because enumerators were barred from entry at military checkpoints, a naive statistical evaluation would falsely penalize the model for a "false positive." Conversely, the model will appear highly skillful in accessible areas where humanitarian footprint and reporting are dense, such as the immediate environs of UN Protection of Civilians (PoC) sites. Therefore, retrospective skill evaluation utilizing humanitarian reporting data in South Sudan is fundamentally unreliable; it inadvertently measures the geography of humanitarian access rather than the precision of the hydrological forecast. This epistemic constraint renders the proposed evaluation design scientifically indefensible.

## The Empirical Basis of the Logistics and Pre-positioning Premise

The operational justification for the proposed decision-support tool rests squarely on the logistics premise that early dry-season pre-positioning—guided by the tool's forecasts—will allow NGOs to avoid a 6-7x wet-season air transport premium. While the stark cost disparity between road and air transport in South Sudan is empirically supported and represents a massive drain on humanitarian finances, the assumption that an NGO requires a predictive tool to trigger this pre-positioning, or that the financial savings directly accrue to a small NGO's bottom line, is contradicted by the structural mechanics of the humanitarian supply chain.

Due to the absence of a functional railway system (which has been completely non-operational since April 2023) and a severely underdeveloped road network, South Sudan heavily relies on the Logistics Cluster (managed by the World Food Programme) to coordinate the movement of virtually all relief items. The Logistics Cluster already implements a rigorous, formalized "Logistics Efficiency and Pre-positioning Strategy" (documented extensively across the 2019-2020 and 2020-2021 cycles and regularly updated).

The fundamental doctrine of this strategy is that all humanitarian actors must pre-position cargo during the dry season, irrespective of any specific meteorological forecast. It is an accepted structural reality that during the May-to-October rainy season, the vast majority of the country's unpaved roads will wash out, become impassable, or be deemed too dangerous for heavy transport, while simultaneously, Haboob sandstorms and dangerous cloud formations further disrupt the limited transport networks. Thus, NGOs do not require a probabilistic flood forecast to decide whether to pre-position in May or June; dry-season pre-positioning is an unconditional, baseline operational requirement explicitly dictated by the Logistics Cluster.

Furthermore, when roads inevitably become impassable and air transport becomes necessary, the exorbitant costs are largely absorbed by the Logistics Cluster and the United Nations Humanitarian Air Service (UNHAS), which manage bulk air operations, consolidate cargo, and are funded through system-wide Programme Support and Administrative (PSA) budgets. Smaller consortium NGOs (e.g., ZOA, Dorcas, Help a Child) rarely charter their own independent cargo flights; instead, they coordinate with the Logistics Cluster to access common transport assets for lighter items. While avoiding air transport is undeniably a system-wide financial imperative, the notion that a localized decision-support tool will allow a small INGO to unilaterally realize a 6-7x cost saving reflects a profound misunderstanding of how the coordinated humanitarian logistics architecture functions. The Logistics Cluster already dictates the temporal rhythm of cargo movement, rendering forecast-conditioned pre-positioning by individual NGOs entirely redundant.

## Fundamental Data Quality and Epistemic Constraints

The integrity and ultimate utility of the proposed tool are heavily dependent on the quality of its underlying geospatial inputs. The reliance on MODIS/VIIRS optical flood masks, WorldPop demographic estimates, and ASAP cropland layers introduces severe technical limitations that degrade the tool's utility in the specific geographic and demographic context of South Sudan.

First, optical satellite sensors such as MODIS and VIIRS are severely compromised in equatorial wetlands during the rainy season. Convective clouds, which are pervasive during the peak rains, completely obscure the land surface. The phenomenon of "veiled cores" in deep convective clouds means that sunlight scattered into and out of these regions provides zero usable information regarding the underlying surface. This leads to catastrophic data gaps exactly when the flood hazard is most acute. Additionally, optical sensors struggle profoundly to distinguish between permanent marshland, seasonal swamps, and genuinely anomalous floodwaters in the highly vegetated Sudd. Accurate flood mapping in this region strictly requires active microwave sensors, such as C-band Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) from Sentinel-1, which can penetrate cloud cover and differentiate water beneath vegetation canopies.

Second, the use of WorldPop for disaggregated demographic exposure is highly precarious in South Sudan. The country has not conducted a comprehensive national population and housing census since 2008, prior to its independence. WorldPop’s methodology in South Sudan attempts to project this drastically outdated 2008 baseline forward, while applying complex adjustments for internal displacement utilizing IOM DTM baseline assessment data. However, this two-step adjustment is fraught with extreme uncertainty. Displacement sites in South Sudan frequently lack defined spatial boundaries, forcing modelers to assume arbitrary maximum capacity caps (e.g., 500 people per grid cell) and buffer areas that may wildly misrepresent the true density of informal settlements. Furthermore, an estimated 1.9 million internally displaced persons (IDPs) and extensive refugee populations fluctuate continuously due to compounding shocks, making static spatial mapping nearly impossible. Consequently, intersecting a highly uncertain, modeled population grid with an obscured optical flood mask results in exposure estimates that possess margins of error far too vast to support targeted, localized operational decisions.

Finally, the utilization of the ASAP (Anomaly Hotspots of Agricultural Production) cropland layer is inadequate for the region's specific agricultural typology. ASAP is a highly effective platform developed by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC) for monitoring large-scale mechanized agriculture and regional anomaly detection, frequently utilized to monitor massive land matrix deals (e.g., thousands of hectares of irrigated sugarcane). However, South Sudanese agriculture is predominantly characterized by smallholder, subsistence-level, shifting farming systems. The relatively coarse spatial resolution of global/regional cropland masks (often ~500m to 1km) fails to capture the fragmented, small-plot cultivation patterns prevalent in the region. Highly localized, high-resolution analyses (incorporating Sentinel-2 imagery) are required to accurately map shifting smallholder exposure, making standard, off-the-shelf aggregate layers insufficient for the proposed level of county prioritization.

## Conclusions and Defensible Alternative Research Pathways

The comprehensive evaluation of the proposed county-level, exposure-coupled flood decision-support tool demonstrates that while the concept is technically sophisticated in its geospatial integration, it fails to provide genuine added value to NGOs operating in South Sudan. The analysis yields the following definitive conclusions regarding the tool's foundational assumptions:

1. **Redundancy (Duplicative Parts):** The proposed spatial intersections and severity rankings directly duplicate existing, operationally integrated, and highly resourced products from OCHA (HNO), UNOSAT, REACH, FAO DIEM, and the IPC. The humanitarian constraint is a lack of funding and political access, not a lack of spatial prioritization tools.
2. **NGO Decision-Making (Unsupported Assumptions):** INGOs (like the DRA) do not possess the institutional agility to alter their geographic footprint annually based on a forecast. Operations are structurally locked into multi-year strategic cycles dictated by donor agreements, Grand Bargain localization targets, and sub-grant partnerships.
3. **Hazard Predictability (Unsupported Assumptions):** The use of 2-3 month seasonal rainfall outlooks is hydrologically invalid for the Sudd wetland, which operates on a verified 16.84-month transit delay from Lake Victoria. Furthermore, the multi-year memory of the wetland means exposure is highly persistent year-to-year, entirely negating the need for dynamic annual re-ranking.
4. **Evaluation Validity (Unsupported Assumptions):** Retrospective skill evaluation against humanitarian datasets (DTM, OCHA) is fatally flawed, as these datasets measure the geography of negotiated humanitarian access and military conflict rather than the true physical distribution of the flood hazard.
5. **Logistics Premise (Unsupported Assumptions):** Dry-season pre-positioning is an established, mandatory structural requirement dictated by the Logistics Cluster due to the May-October rainy season, not a novel strategy requiring predictive algorithms.
6. **Data Quality Constraints:** The reliance on optical imagery (MODIS/VIIRS) in a cloudy equatorial zone, combined with WorldPop data rooted in a 2008 pre-independence census and coarse ASAP cropland data, introduces unacceptable levels of epistemic uncertainty that degrade operational utility.



### Defensible Alternative Research Contributions (Novel Pathways)

Rather than engineering a redundant, operationally incompatible short-term targeting tool, researchers operating in this problem space should pivot toward structural and long-term diagnostic analyses that align with the true hydrodynamics and political economy of South Sudan.

A defensible and highly useful research contribution would leverage the recently established 17-month transit time of the Victoria-Kyoga-Albert-Sudd cascade to model long-term, multi-year infrastructural risk. Because the system exhibits immense hydrodynamic memory, research should focus on predicting long-term shifts in permanent wetland boundaries. This extended lead time (over a year) is perfectly suited to guide the placement of durable humanitarian assets, such as elevating airstrips, fortifying primary supply routes, and relocating semi-permanent IDP camps out of expanding floodplains.

Additionally, future geospatial research should pivot from predictive prioritization to retroactive accountability. By combining Sentinel-1 SAR flood mapping with highly localized settlement data, researchers could analyze the historical intersection of climate shocks with conflict-driven access denial. This would provide the humanitarian community with empirical, irrefutable evidence to advocate against the weaponization of aid access by armed actors. Grounding geospatial research in the structural, multi-year, and political realities of South Sudan will yield significantly higher operational utility than redundant seasonal prioritization algorithms.
