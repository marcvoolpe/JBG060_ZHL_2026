Empirical Validation of Satellite Flood-Exposure Metrics Against Ground-Truth Humanitarian Impact in South Sudan: A Critical Econometric AppraisalThe operational deployment of satellite-derived flood exposure metrics has fundamentally restructured humanitarian information management. Across Sub-Saharan Africa, and acutely within the Republic of South Sudan, geographic information systems (GIS) are utilized to overlay surface water masks onto gridded population and agricultural datasets. The resulting figures are subsequently published on dashboards, disseminated in situational reports, and utilized to trigger multi-million-dollar Anticipatory Action frameworks. However, the theoretical validity, empirical robustness, and econometric stability of this "exposure overlay" paradigm remain largely untested in complex, conflict-affected, and hydrologically unique environments.This comprehensive appraisal evaluates a proposed Master's/Capstone research objective designed to empirically test whether coupling satellite flood hazard masks with high-resolution demographic and agricultural grids provides measurable explanatory value for observed humanitarian impacts in South Sudan between 2019 and 2025. By systematically deconstructing the theoretical precedent, the structural limitations of ground-truth data, the pervasive threat of assessment bias, and the econometric feasibility of the proposed analytical framework, this report provides an exhaustive blueprint for rigorous empirical validation.1. Literature and Theoretical PrecedentThe foundational epistemology of humanitarian geospatial analysis rests on a linear assumption of risk: physical exposure to a hazard correlates proportionally with humanitarian impact. In pixel-based geospatial operations, risk is operationalized by multiplying binary or probabilistic inundation masks by continuous demographic rasters or agricultural layers. While this methodology has demonstrated high predictive validity in stationary, highly urbanized populations with well-documented census baselines, contemporary disaster science literature increasingly questions its uncritical application in pastoralist, semi-nomadic, and wetland environments.The Spatial Mismatch and Compensatory Dilution ParadigmsIn Sub-Saharan Africa, the exposure overlay paradigm is highly susceptible to phenomena characterized as "compensatory dilution" and "spatial mismatch." The Sudd wetland in South Sudan represents a dynamic, pulsating ecological system where inundation is historically chronic rather than acute. Indigenous communities residing along the White Nile and within the Sudd exhibit profound chronic adaptation to seasonal water fluctuations. Mechanisms such as elevated dike-building, canoe-based transport, and strategic cattle mobility ensure that the presence of surface water does not inherently trigger catastrophic displacement or acute humanitarian need. Conversely, when sudden, shallow inundation breaches unadapted pastoral or equatorial zones, the destruction of infrastructure precipitates immediate, severe crises.The uncritical application of off-the-shelf demographic grids exacerbates this spatial mismatch. Global datasets such as WorldPop rely on machine learning algorithms—frequently random forests—that disaggregate national population estimates based on covariates such as nighttime lights, distance to roads, and building footprints. In South Sudan, the last national census was conducted in 2008, prior to independence and multiple civil wars. Consequently, these models distribute populations with a high degree of false precision. When a satellite detects flooding in a wetland, the hazard mask frequently intersects with demographic pixels that contain modeled populations but are, in reality, seasonal swamps devoid of permanent human settlement. This statistical intersection generates hyper-inflated exposure figures that introduce noise rather than actionable signal.Empirical literature testing the predictive relationship between satellite-modeled flood exposure and observed humanitarian outcomes in fragile contexts remains alarmingly sparse. While Earth Observation (EO) studies frequently document the expansion of flooded areas—noting, for instance, that flood extent across major African basins is expanding rapidly and driving human displacement—they rarely validate their exposure indices against verified ground-truth humanitarian databases. Studies integrating satellite imagery with community-based participatory mapping in regions like the White Volta Basin in Ghana have demonstrated that localized vulnerability knowledge dramatically outperforms purely remote-sensing proxies in predicting displacement and prioritizing aid. Similarly, deep learning applications mapping adequate housing indices across floodplains indicate that high-resolution vulnerability data is essential, yet often missing, for accurate impact estimation.The proposed research question—whether complex exposure indices add predictive value over raw hazard extent or historical persistence—addresses a critical, unresolved gap in humanitarian geospatial research. If raw inundation area or simple historical flood frequency outperforms complex demographic overlays in explaining actual displacement, it fundamentally challenges the technical architecture of current humanitarian needs assessments.2. Ground-Truth Data Availability and Granularity in South SudanTo construct a valid econometric evaluation comparing exposure metrics against baselines, the dependent variable—humanitarian impact—must be rigorously defined, consistently sourced, and spatially granular. The South Sudanese operational context presents profound challenges regarding data fidelity, temporal consistency, and attribution. Evaluating the available ground-truth datasets reveals significant structural limitations that must be navigated.The Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) DynamicsThe International Organization for Migration (IOM) Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) serves as the primary instrument for quantifying population movement in fragile states. However, leveraging DTM data as a dependent variable for flood impact requires careful methodological disambiguation. DTM employs multiple distinct mechanisms: Event Tracking, which records sudden-onset displacements; Baseline Assessments, which provide periodic geographical snapshots of internally displaced persons (IDPs) and returnees; and Mobility Tracking, which monitors population flows at key transit nodes.Event Tracking provides the highest temporal fidelity for isolated flood shocks but suffers from spatial clustering and irregular geographic coverage. Furthermore, disentangling "flood-driven" from "conflict-driven" displacement in South Sudan is analytically perilous. During the 2019–2025 period, sub-national violence in states like Jonglei, Unity, and Upper Nile frequently coincided with the rainy season and severe flooding. While DTM explicitly categorizes the primary trigger for displacement in its event tracking, compound crises generate multi-causal flight. A population may initially relocate locally due to flooding but experience secondary, long-range displacement due to intercommunal violence exacerbated by shrinking dry-land grazing areas.The spatial unit of aggregation poses another econometric challenge. DTM data is often collected at the payam (admin3) or specific site level. Aggregating this high-resolution data to the county level (admin2) to match standard UN spatial reporting introduces ecological fallacies, obscuring hyper-local vulnerability where micro-topography dictates survival. Moreover, temporal aggregation (e.g., matching daily satellite passes to quarterly DTM mobility tracking) risks smoothing out the acute shock dynamics that the econometric model aims to detect.OCHA Flood Dashboards and the Definition of "Affected"The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) regularly publishes Inter-Agency Rapid Needs Assessments (IRNAs) and flood dashboards detailing "affected" populations. While ubiquitous in humanitarian reporting, this dataset is highly problematic for rigorous econometric modeling. The term "affected" lacks a standardized, physiologically, or economically objective threshold. In practice, it functions as a political and operational construct, representing a negotiated consensus among humanitarian clusters rather than a strictly empirical measurement.Furthermore, OCHA data is heavily skewed by the operational footprint of humanitarian partners. Counties with a high density of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) are more likely to execute rapid assessments and report detailed affected figures, whereas marginalized or logistically isolated counties often default to zero or carry forward outdated demographic estimates. Treating a reported zero as a true zero in OCHA datasets inevitably biases regression coefficients downward, punishing the satellite model for correctly identifying flooding in areas where the humanitarian system simply failed to measure the impact.Confounding within the IPC FrameworkThe Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) offers county-level estimates of acute food insecurity (Phase 3+), representing a highly standardized metric of humanitarian crisis. However, utilizing IPC as a primary ground-truth proxy for flood impact is fraught with massive confounding variables. The 2019–2025 era witnessed cascading macroeconomic shocks in South Sudan, including the severe depreciation of the South Sudanese Pound, hyperinflation, localized market collapses, and the total disruption of cross-border trade due to the concurrent armed conflict in Sudan. The localized signal of flood-induced crop failure is frequently overwhelmed by these macroeconomic variables. Additionally, IPC classifications incorporate anticipated humanitarian food assistance (HFA) into their projections. If a highly flooded county receives massive airdrops or prepositioned supplies, its IPC classification may artificially stabilize, breaking the statistical correlation between flood exposure and acute food insecurity impact.FAO DIEM and Agricultural Loss DataThe Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Data in Emergencies (DIEM) and Crop and Food Security Assessment Missions (CFSAM) attempt to quantify agricultural losses. However, empirical, county-level crop harvest loss or livestock mortality data that is directly measured on the ground—rather than modeled based on the very same satellite vegetative indices being tested—is exceedingly rare in South Sudan. Most agricultural impact figures are derivative models. Utilizing modeled agricultural loss as a ground-truth dependent variable to test a modeled satellite exposure independent variable results in tautological analysis, capturing the correlation between two overlapping algorithms rather than ground reality.Alternative Epidemiological Proxies: DHIS2 and Cholera IncidenceGiven the profound limitations of displacement, food security, and subjective assessment data, epidemiological indicators offer a compelling, high-frequency alternative for impact validation. The District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2), managed by the Ministry of Health with support from the Health Pooled Fund and the World Bank, tracks facility-level morbidity, mortality, and service utilization across South Sudan. While the DHIS2 system faces challenges regarding data culture and infrastructure, it represents a systematically collected, empirical dataset.Severe flooding predictably submerges health infrastructure, leading to measurable, objective drop-offs in DHIS2 reporting completeness and timeliness. For example, late 2024 reporting data explicitly noted that inundation across 58 health facilities directly caused turbulent declines in national IDSR (Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response) completeness. Utilizing anomalous drops in facility reporting rates as a dependent variable offers an objective measure of physical flood impact.Furthermore, acute waterborne diseases provide a physiological proxy for flood impact. Flooding contaminates boreholes, compromises fragile sanitation infrastructure, and triggers explosive outbreaks of acute watery diarrhea and cholera. The 2024–2025 cholera outbreaks in Renk, Malakal, and Juba provide stark evidence of the epidemiological impact of extreme weather events intersecting with forced displacement and poor WASH infrastructure.Similarly, the disruption of maternal health services serves as a powerful indicator of humanitarian impact. The "Three Delays" model of maternal mortality—delay in seeking care, delay in reaching a facility, and delay in receiving quality care—is acutely exacerbated by flooding. When floodwaters sever road networks, pregnant women are forced to rely on canoes for journeys exceeding 12 hours, severely compounding the second delay (reaching a facility) and leading to preventable maternal and neonatal morbidity. The inability to maintain cold chains for essential uterotonics like oxytocin during floods further paralyzes the health system, though the introduction of heat-stable carbetocin by UNFPA and WHO aims to mitigate this specific logistical failure. By utilizing spikes in cholera incidence, or facility-level data on unattended births and maternal complications, researchers can construct impact metrics that are grounded in physiological reality rather than subjective humanitarian negotiation.3. The Assessment and Access Bias TrapThe most severe methodological threat to this research project is the non-random missingness and potential endogeneity of the dependent variables. Humanitarian data is not generated through probability sampling; it is a direct function of physical, logistical, and political access. Failure to explicitly model and control for these systemic biases will invalidate any subsequent regression analysis.Selection Bias and Logistical Access DenialIn South Sudan, the geography of humanitarian data is inextricably linked to the geography of logistics. During the rainy season, wadis (dry riverbeds) fill rapidly, and up to 60% of the unimproved road network becomes entirely impassable. If a county experiences catastrophic flooding, the physical infrastructure connecting it to humanitarian hubs is severed, physically preventing inter-agency assessment teams from reaching the affected populations.Consequently, the most severely impacted areas frequently record zero assessed victims simply because they are inaccessible. Simultaneously, political access denial by state and non-state armed actors restricts humanitarian movement, creating administrative black holes. If unassessed counties are coded as having zero displacement or zero affected populations in the econometric model, the regression will perversely penalize the satellite flood masks for accurately identifying highly flooded areas that the humanitarian sector failed to assess.To mitigate this, researchers must deploy statistical techniques designed for non-random sample selection. The Heckman two-step selection model is highly appropriate. The first stage (the selection equation) models the probability that a county is assessed at all, utilizing exclusionary instruments that affect access but not necessarily the underlying flood vulnerability. Appropriate instruments include the physical distance to the nearest UNMISS logistics base, the presence of all-weather airstrips, or OCHA's quarterly access constraint scoring matrix. The second stage models the magnitude of the humanitarian impact, conditioning on the inverse Mills ratio derived from the first stage to mathematically correct for the access bias.Circularity and Assessment ContaminationAn even more insidious form of bias is assessment contamination, or circularity. In modern humanitarian operations, assessment teams explicitly utilize satellite flood maps—often the exact UNOSAT, REACH, or WFP products being evaluated by this research—to determine which counties to prioritize for rapid needs assessments. If a geospatial dashboard indicates that a specific county has 50,000 flood-exposed individuals, humanitarian coordinators are highly likely to deploy a team to that specific county to verify the impact. The assessment team then interviews local authorities and publishes an impact figure closely mirroring the initial satellite estimate.If the dependent variable (observed impact) is causally determined by the independent variable (satellite exposure estimate) through the mechanism of assessment targeting, the resulting statistical correlation is entirely spurious. It represents an endogenous loop of self-fulfilling prophecies, measuring the influence of the map on the humanitarian system rather than the accuracy of the map regarding the physical world. To test for and mitigate this circularity, the research must utilize instrumental variable (IV) regression or exploit natural experiments. Alternatively, researchers could subset the data, evaluating the predictive power of satellite metrics strictly in regions where assessment targeting was explicitly documented to have been independent of geospatial early warning triggers, or utilize the aforementioned epidemiological data (which is collected routinely regardless of satellite triggers) as the ground truth.4. Methodological and Econometric FeasibilityTo execute a rigorous, publishable empirical test, the research must formalize a statistical "horse race" evaluating whether complex demographic overlays provide marginal explanatory value over simple hazard geometries or historical constants. The unit of analysis is the county-month (or county-year, depending on the temporal fidelity of the impact data), yielding an unbalanced panel dataset of approximately 78 counties over 84 months ($N \approx 78, T \approx 84$), resulting in roughly 6,500 potential observations, albeit with substantial missingness.Formulating the Statistical Horse RaceThe evaluation framework should compare a hierarchy of models, escalating in geospatial complexity to isolate the marginal value of each data layer.Model DesignationExposure Construct FormulationTheoretical Rationale & HypothesisBaseline 1: Static Persistence$Historical\ Frequency_{i}$Tests whether long-term vulnerability and chronic flood risk explain impact better than dynamic satellite tracking. Furthermore, the dataset will contain a high frequency of structural zeros. Therefore, linear Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression is theoretically inappropriate and will yield biased standard errors and impossible negative predictions.The research must employ a count data framework, specifically a Fixed-Effects Negative Binomial model or a Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood (PPML) estimator. The baseline econometric specification can be expressed as:$$E[Y_{it} \vert{} X_{it}, c_i, \tau_t] = \exp(\beta_1 Exposure_{it} + \mathbf{\theta} \mathbf{Z}_{it} + c_i + \tau_t)$$Where:$Y_{it}$ represents the observed humanitarian impact in county $i$ at time $t$.$Exposure_{it}$ is the candidate satellite metric being evaluated (e.g., WorldPop exposure vs. Raw Area).$\mathbf{Z}_{it}$ represents a vector of time-varying confounders. Given the context, this must include high-resolution conflict event data (e.g., ACLED fatality counts) to control for displacement driven by armed violence, as well as baseline precipitation metrics to control for general rainy season dynamics independent of specific localized flooding.$c_i$ represents county-level fixed effects. This controls for unobserved, time-invariant heterogeneity, such as baseline poverty, static topography, cultural adaptation to water, or chronic vulnerability.$\tau_t$ represents time fixed effects to absorb nationwide macroeconomic shocks, seasonal norms, or secular trends (e.g., the national depreciation of the currency).The inclusion of county fixed effects ($c_i$) is mathematically vital. It isolates the within-county variance, answering the precise causal question: "When flood exposure in County X increases beyond its normal historical baseline, does humanitarian impact in County X simultaneously increase?" This eliminates the risk of cross-sectional confounding, where intrinsically vulnerable or highly populated counties simply report higher impacts regardless of specific flood events.Advanced Evaluation Metrics and Machine LearningTo assess predictive skill rather than mere in-sample correlation, the models must be evaluated using rigorous scoring rules. Relying solely on $R^2$ or coefficient p-values is insufficient for operational validation. Brier scores, Continuous Ranked Probability Scores (CRPS), and out-of-sample Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) using k-fold cross-validation will explicitly quantify the marginal predictive value added by the WorldPop or ASAP overlays compared to the raw inundation baseline. For ranking prioritization—answering whether the models correctly identify the "top 10 worst-hit counties"—Spearman rank correlations and Top-K overlap metrics should be deployed.Furthermore, because flood displacement involves populations moving across administrative boundaries, the integration of Spatial Durbin Models (SDM) or spatial lag terms ($\rho \mathbf{W} Y_t$) must be considered to account for spatial autocorrelation and spillover effects. Advanced hydrological forecasting frameworks, such as the two-stage INFLOW-AI model recently deployed in South Sudan, provide contemporary benchmarks for predicting extreme seasonal flood extents. INFLOW-AI utilizes transformer-based models and ConvLSTM architectures to capture non-linear hydrometeorological patterns over a 60-day lead time. Comparing the econometric output of standard GIS overlays against the predictive output of these advanced machine learning frameworks will contextualize the technological frontier of humanitarian data science.5. Stakeholder Utility and Decision ValueA central question in applied data science is the operational utility of a potential null finding. Suppose the econometric analysis definitively confirms the skeptical hypothesis: Off-the-shelf satellite exposure grids (Hazard × WorldPop) add zero, or even negative, statistically significant predictive skill for observed humanitarian impact over simple raw flood extent and historical persistence.The Strategic Value of a Null ResultFar from being a failed research endeavor, a rigorously defended null result would represent a paradigm-shifting insight for ZOA, the Dutch Relief Alliance (DRA), and the broader humanitarian sector. Currently, millions of dollars in climate adaptation and emergency funding are triggered by predictive models heavily reliant on these exact exposure dashboards. In May 2022, for example, the Central Emergency Response Fund (CERF) and the South Sudan Humanitarian Fund (SSHF) allocated $19 million in Anticipatory Action funding to Unity State, specifically targeting Bentiu, based on predictive models and early warnings of catastrophic flooding.These funds were utilized to reinforce 55 kilometers of dikes, distribute cash grants, and pre-position supplies before the flood peak. While the anticipatory intervention was broadly deemed successful, the underlying trigger mechanisms depend heavily on the accuracy of geospatial exposure estimates. If this research proves that multiplying hazard masks by outdated demographic grids introduces false precision and noise, it fundamentally invalidates the technocratic assumption that combining two flawed global datasets yields actionable local intelligence. It suggests that Anticipatory Action triggers might be misallocating pre-arranged finance based on algorithmic artifacts rather than genuine ground vulnerability.For ZOA and DRA, this finding would justify a strategic pivot away from centralized, satellite-driven macro-targeting toward deeply localized, community-based vulnerability assessments. It would provide empirical leverage to challenge donor requirements that demand high-resolution geospatial exposure figures in grant proposals, arguing instead that such figures are epistemologically bankrupt in contexts like the Sudd wetland. Furthermore, it would redirect information management resources away from updating complex GIS population overlays and toward maintaining simple, high-frequency raw inundation monitoring combined with localized early warning networks.Fatal Flaws, Failure Modes, and Red FlagsBefore embarking on the full execution of this research, several critical red flags must be navigated to prevent the analytical collapse of the study:The Zero-Inflation Trap: If the dependent variable matrix consists of 85-90% zeros purely due to lack of assessment access or reporting failures, standard panel regressions will fail to converge or will produce highly biased coefficients. The Heckman selection correction or Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) models are not optional robustness checks; they are mandatory for model survival.Collinearity in Exposure Metrics: Raw inundation area and WorldPop-weighted inundation area are geometrically linked and likely to be highly collinear ($r > 0.85$). Attempting to include Baseline 2 and Model 1 in the same regression equation will cause severe variance inflation. The models must be evaluated sequentially in a non-nested framework using information criteria (AIC/BIC) or out-of-sample cross-validation to compare their independent explanatory power.Temporal Mismatch and Lag Structures: Satellite imagery provides near real-time, instantaneous snapshots (e.g., daily MODIS, bi-weekly Sentinel-1 SAR), whereas humanitarian impact data is often published quarterly or annually without precise onset dates. Reconciling this temporal granularity is complex. Averaging daily flood pixels into quarterly blocks smooths out the acute shock dynamics, potentially destroying the very variance the econometrician is attempting to measure. Distributed lag models must be employed to capture the delayed onset of displacement and epidemiological outbreaks following a flood peak.Ignoring the Conflict Confounder: Analyzing South Sudanese displacement and food insecurity without explicitly modeling armed conflict is an exercise in omitted variable bias. A surge in displacement during the rainy season could be entirely driven by seasonal cattle-raiding cycles, political offensives, or military maneuvering rather than the rising water itself. High-resolution conflict data must be integrated into the right-hand side of the equation as a dynamic control variable to isolate the independent effect of the flood hazard.The empirical validation of satellite flood-exposure metrics against ground-truth humanitarian impact in South Sudan represents an ambitious, highly necessary intervention. By applying rigorous econometric techniques, including fixed-effects panel regressions and selection bias corrections, this research has the potential to quantify the exact degree of false precision introduced by global population grids. Navigating the severe data limitations of humanitarian reporting requires advanced statistical care, but if executed correctly, the findings will offer immense strategic value. Whether validating the use of sophisticated geospatial dashboards or proving that simple raw hazard geometry serves as a superior proxy, the results will directly inform the optimization of Anticipatory Action triggers, reshape donor funding allocations, and anchor humanitarian information management in empirical reality rather than technological assumption.
