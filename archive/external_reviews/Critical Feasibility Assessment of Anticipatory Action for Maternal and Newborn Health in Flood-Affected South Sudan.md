Critical Feasibility Assessment of Anticipatory Action for Maternal and Newborn Health in Flood-Affected South Sudan1. Executive Verdict: Defensibility of the Proposed Capstone ObjectiveFollowing an exhaustive evaluation of the clinical, logistical, organizational, and data science realities in South Sudan, the verdict on pursuing the proposed capstone objective—predicting maternal and newborn health (MNH) deliveries cut off by floods to trigger anticipatory supply pre-positioning—is a definitive NO-GO.While the humanitarian motivation underpinning the project is fundamentally sound, the core premise suffers from multiple fatal flaws that render it scientifically unviable as an academic data science capstone and operationally irrelevant for a generalist non-governmental organization (NGO) consortium such as the ZOA / Dutch Relief Alliance (DRA). The proposed project relies heavily on an assumption that a lack of physical medical supplies at primary health facilities during seasonal Sudd floods is the primary bottleneck driving maternal mortality. However, deep clinical evidence, humanitarian operations data, and epidemiological models indicate that the true binding constraints are the physical inability of women in labor to reach flooded facilities, the chronic absence of skilled human resources for health (HRH), and the collapse of the referral system for emergency obstetric care. Pre-positioning advanced surgical or delivery kits inside a flooded, unstaffed primary health care unit (PHCU) that pregnant women cannot traverse inundated terrain to reach will not avert maternal death.Furthermore, from a strict data science and academic feasibility perspective, the project faces an insurmountable evaluability crisis. The District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) platform in South Sudan exhibits severe spatial and temporal reporting gaps that are perfectly correlated with, and often caused by, the flood hazard itself. A machine learning model predicting "cut-off deliveries" cannot be validated against ground-truth outcome data if the health facilities in the inundated zones simultaneously lose their physical and digital capacity to report to the DHIS2 system during the exact months under analysis.Consequently, to salvage the immense utility of the existing INFLOW-AI flood forecasts—which boast a highly valuable 2-month (6-dekad) lead time and a 252-meter spatial resolution—the capstone team must pivot. The focus must shift to a sector where predictive spatial data directly aligns with both verifiable ground-truth outcomes and the operational mandates of generalist NGOs. The strongest alternative pivot is an Anticipatory WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene) strategy for Cholera and Acute Watery Diarrhea (AWD) Prevention. This entails predicting the inundation of critical point-source water infrastructure (such as boreholes and latrines) to trigger localized chlorination, borehole elevation, and targeted hygiene kit distribution before physical access closes.2. The Clinical and Logistical Reality of MNH in Flood SettingsTo understand why pre-positioning MNH supplies fails to address the root cause of maternal mortality during floods, it is necessary to examine the clinical architecture of obstetric care in South Sudan through the established "Three Delays" model of maternal mortality, alongside an assessment of the country's severe workforce limitations.2.1 The "Three Delays" Model in the Context of the Sudd WetlandsSouth Sudan has one of the highest maternal mortality ratios (MMR) globally, currently estimated between 789 and 1,150 deaths per 100,000 live births. Only 19% of births are attended by skilled health personnel, and the vast majority of deliveries occur at home, placing immense risk on mothers experiencing complications. The "Three Delays" framework categorizes the systemic drivers of these deaths into three distinct, chronological phases: the delay in deciding to seek care (First Delay), the delay in reaching a health facility (Second Delay), and the delay in receiving appropriate emergency care upon arrival at the facility (Third Delay).The proposed capstone assumes that addressing the Third Delay—specifically via the anticipatory provision of physical supplies at the facility—will yield a measurable reduction in maternal mortality. However, the realities of seasonal flooding in South Sudan primarily exacerbate the First and Second Delays to an extent that negates the utility of facility-based supply interventions.The First Delay is deeply entrenched in socioeconomic deprivation, which is compounded by displacement. In flooded internally displaced person (IDP) camps and rural settlements, the loss of livelihoods and the destruction of agriculture severely weaken household purchasing power. Even if maternal health services are nominally free, the indirect costs of seeking care—such as hiring specialized transport or paying for diagnostic tests—become insurmountable for families whose assets have been washed away.The Second Delay represents the most absolute physical barrier. When extreme seasonal floods inundate the Sudd wetlands and surrounding regions across Unity, Jonglei, and Upper Nile states, road networks effectively disappear. Communities become isolated on shrinking islands surrounded by deep, stagnant water. Pregnant women in obstructed labor, or those experiencing severe post-partum hemorrhage (PPH), are forced to travel by makeshift canoes for up to 12 hours to reach functioning health centers. If a primary health care center (PHCC) is successfully pre-stocked with delivery kits based on a 2-month forecast, but is subsequently surrounded by kilometers of floodwaters, the medical supplies are rendered useless because the catchment population cannot traverse the inundated terrain to utilize them. Supply pre-positioning does not build bridges or provide mechanized amphibious transport.2.2 The Binding Constraint: Human Resources for Health (HRH) vs. SuppliesEven in scenarios where a woman successfully navigates the floodwaters and arrives at a clinic, the mere presence of pre-positioned supplies is rarely the decisive factor in her survival. The ultimate binding constraint in South Sudan is a catastrophic, systemic shortage of skilled human resources for health (HRH).South Sudan possesses a health workforce density of roughly 7.6 per 10,000 population, drastically below the World Health Organization recommended threshold for achieving universal health coverage. The midwifery sector is particularly fragile. At the time of independence in 2011, the country possessed an estimated 12 fully qualified midwives. While international donor efforts, including those backed by the Health Pooled Fund (HPF), have increased this number to over 600 trained midwives, the distribution remains highly uneven, with severe urban bias. Rural and flood-prone facilities frequently rely on task-shifting to under-qualified community health workers who lack the clinical competency to manage complex obstetric emergencies.To manage the 15% of pregnancies that inevitably develop life-threatening complications, the health system must provide Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (CEmONC). CEmONC requires surgical interventions (cesarean sections), safe anesthesia, and emergency blood transfusions. These interventions inherently require surgeons, anesthetists, and a functional operating theater supported by reliable power. A predictive, 2-month anticipatory warning cannot manifest a skilled surgical team in a cut-off rural outpost. Consequently, providing a highly complex Referral Level Surgical Kit to an unstaffed or under-qualified PHCC does not save lives; it merely results in the expiration, abandonment, or looting of expensive medical assets during the chaos of displacement.2.3 Structural Vulnerability of Health FacilitiesThe physical infrastructure of primary healthcare in South Sudan is profoundly vulnerable to climate shocks. During the historic floods of 2021 through 2024, hundreds of health facilities across Jonglei, Unity, and Upper Nile were completely submerged, damaged, or forced to close. In late 2024 alone, an estimated 58 health facilities in flood-affected areas were inundated, severely compromising their ability to function or store medical commodities safely.Pre-positioning fragile, temperature-sensitive medical supplies in mud-brick or semi-permanent structures that are highly likely to flood introduces severe risks of inventory destruction through water damage and mold. Furthermore, escalating intercommunal violence and conflict frequently overlap with flood displacement, leading to the looting and destruction of health infrastructure. Thus, anticipatory pre-positioning in highly vulnerable structural environments carries an unacceptable risk of asset loss.3. A historical barrier to pre-positioning Kit 6 (Clinical Delivery) in rural South Sudanese health posts was the presence of oxytocin, the global gold-standard uterotonic for preventing and treating PPH. Oxytocin requires a strict cold chain (2–8°C) to remain effective. Because flood-prone rural clinics frequently lack reliable electricity or solar refrigeration, oxytocin rapidly degrades in the ambient heat, rendering it useless when an emergency occurs.However, recent global clinical shifts have fundamentally altered this constraint. Following the landmark Carbetocin Haemorrhage Prevention (CHAMPION) trial, the WHO updated its uterotonic guidelines to strongly recommend the use of Heat-Stable Carbetocin (HSC) or oral misoprostol for PPH prevention in settings where the cold chain is unreliable. HSC remains stable at temperatures up to 30°C for at least three years, even in high humidity, and UNFPA has actively integrated it into its essential medicines catalog for low-resource settings.While the introduction of heat-stable carbetocin effectively resolves the thermostability argument against early pre-positioning, it does not resolve the personnel argument. HSC is an injectable uterotonic that must be administered intramuscularly or intravenously by a trained health care provider. If the flood isolates the clinic from the midwife, or if the facility lacks trained staff, the heat-stable drug simply sits unused in the pharmacy. Conversely, while oral misoprostol can be administered by lower-level community workers, it is generally considered less effective than injectable uterotonics for severe PPH. Therefore, the resolution of the cold chain problem does not negate the ultimate HRH bottleneck.4. Critical Fatal Flaws and Data Science ObstaclesBeyond the clinical and logistical mismatches regarding supply provision, the proposed capstone project fails the criteria for a rigorous academic data science endeavor due to profound organizational mandate conflicts and systemic data availability flaws.4.1 Organizational Mandate MismatchThe stakeholder context indicates that the data science modeling output would support humanitarian planning through generalist NGOs like ZOA or the Dutch Relief Alliance (DRA). However, organizations like ZOA focus primarily on Food Security & Livelihoods (FSL), WASH, and peacebuilding. These generalist INGOs do not possess the clinical mandate, the pharmaceutical import licenses, or the specialized medical staff required to manage a complex MNH pipeline.In South Sudan, the health sector is highly siloed and heavily managed. MNH is tightly controlled by the Ministry of Health, UNFPA, the Health Cluster, and specialized health NGOs (e.g., MSF, Medair, International Medical Corps) operating under consolidated financing mechanisms like the Health Pooled Fund (HPF) or World Bank projects. Building a predictive MNH dashboard for an NGO consortium that is legally and operationally barred from procuring surgical obstetric care results in a tool with zero operational utility. Anticipatory action requires pre-agreed financing and execution mandates; providing MNH analytics to a WASH organization breaks this chain of action.4.2 The "Dry Season" Pipeline and Logistics ConstraintsThe humanitarian supply chain in South Sudan is already anticipatory out of sheer geographic necessity, albeit on a macro-seasonal scale rather than an agile 2-month machine learning trigger. The Health Cluster and UNFPA execute a massive "dry season pre-positioning" strategy between December and May, moving bulk core pipeline supplies to county-level hubs while the road networks are passable.An ad-hoc, 2-month INFLOW-AI trigger generated in July (predicting a September flood peak) offers negligible utility for the physical movement of MNH supplies. By July, the arterial dirt roads and wadis (dry riverbeds) in states like Jonglei, Unity, and Upper Nile are frequently already impassable to heavy logistics trucks due to the onset of the rainy season. The logistics window has already closed. Furthermore, procuring IARH kits internationally and clearing them through complex customs procedures in Juba routinely takes three to six months, rendering a 2-month predictive trigger operationally incompatible with global health procurement timelines. Air transport of heavy medical kits (like Kit 11) via the UN Humanitarian Air Service (UNHAS) or WFP helicopters is prohibitively expensive and reserved for acute, life-saving triage rather than bulk anticipatory pre-positioning.4.3 The Endogeneity of DHIS2 Missingness (The Evaluation Crisis)For an academic data science capstone to be scientifically valid, a predictive machine learning model must be evaluated against an observed ground truth. The capstone team intends to use DHIS2 data (e.g., facility deliveries, recorded maternal deaths) to validate the model's predictions of "cut-off deliveries." However, South Sudan's DHIS2 system suffers from chronic reporting failures that are completely endogenous to the flood hazard.When floods inundate a county, the health information architecture collapses alongside the physical infrastructure. Health workers are displaced, solar panels powering VSAT internet connections are submerged, and the physical transport of paper tally sheets from rural clinics to the County Health Department (CHD) for digital entry becomes impossible. Routine WHO Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) bulletins explicitly document this phenomenon, noting that reporting completeness and timeliness plummet in flooded regions (e.g., timeliness dropping below 50% in EWARN mobile sites during peak crises). Furthermore, an estimated 20% of mapped health facilities in South Sudan are classified as "ghost facilities"—clinics that are closed, unstaffed, or destroyed, yet still linger in the national Master Facility List.If the capstone team builds a model to predict flood-induced delivery disruptions, they will inevitably find that the outcome variable (recorded deliveries in DHIS2) drops to zero in flooded areas. The data science team will be fundamentally unable to statistically distinguish whether this drop to zero occurred because (A) the women were trapped and delivered at home, (B) the facility was physically submerged and destroyed, or (C) the facility remained fully operational but the internet or paper transport failed, preventing DHIS2 data entry.An academic model that cannot be mathematically evaluated against a clean, unbiased test set will fail standard data science rubrics. It risks devolving into an unvalidated, descriptive "dashboard calculation" (e.g., multiplying an unverified WorldPop demographic raster by a 4% CBR) rather than a rigorous, testable predictive algorithm.5. If Pursued: A Rigorous Research Objective FormulationIf the academic supervisors mandate the continuation of the MNH focus despite the aforementioned fatal flaws, the research objective must be radically narrowed to focus purely on spatial accessibility rather than clinical outcomes. The objective must adhere to strict academic criteria:Research Objective:To quantify the localized impact of seasonal flooding on maternal healthcare accessibility in South Sudan by developing a dynamic, friction-based spatial model that integrates 252m-resolution INFLOW-AI inundation forecasts with OpenStreetMap road networks and the WHO Master Health Facility List, to estimate the changing travel-time to nearest functional EmONC facilities for women of reproductive age over a 6-dekad forecast horizon.Methodological Components:Action: Model the deterioration of physical accessibility to health facilities.Outcome: A dynamic catchment area analysis showing the population of women physically cut off from care.Scope: Selected highly flood-prone counties (e.g., Rubkona in Unity State or Bor South in Jonglei) with ground-truthed facility locations.Inputs: INFLOW-AI 2-month inundation rasters, WorldPop age-sex disaggregated demographics (females 15–49), Malaria Atlas Project friction surfaces, and OSM infrastructure.Baseline: Dry-season travel time to the nearest health facility.Performance Metric (Validation): Cannot be validated against clinical outcomes due to DHIS2 failures. Must be structurally validated against WFP Logistics Cluster road closure maps and satellite imagery of inundated road segments.Stakeholder Purpose: To provide the Health Cluster and UNHAS with targeted spatial data indicating where mobile clinics, helicopter medical evacuations, or amphibious community outreach teams must be prioritized.6. If Discarded: The Strongest Alternative PivotGiven the 252-meter spatial resolution and the 2-month (6-dekad) lead time of the INFLOW-AI model, the capstone team possesses an exceptionally high-quality hazard input. To salvage the project, the objective must pivot to a humanitarian sector that aligns with ZOA/DRA's operational mandate, benefits from high spatial resolution, and possesses verifiable ground-truth outcome data. The optimal pivot is Anticipatory WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene) Interventions for Cholera and Acute Watery Diarrhea (AWD) Mitigation.6.1 The Epidemiological Link Between Floods and CholeraIn the context of the Sudd wetlands, extreme flooding directly correlates with explosive outbreaks of waterborne diseases. The inundation of open defecation fields, the collapse of poorly constructed pit latrines, and the submersion of unprotected community boreholes result in the rapid, widespread contamination of drinking water with Vibrio cholerae. In late 2024 and continuing into 2025, a massive cholera outbreak spread through flood-affected regions including Renk, Malakal, and Juba, resulting in tens of thousands of suspected cases and over 1,400 deaths.6.2 Alignment with Anticipatory Action and Generalist NGO MandatesUnlike surgical maternal health, WASH interventions are highly suited to Anticipatory Action and represent the core competency of generalist NGOs like ZOA. Evidence from South Sudan proves that this model works. During the 2022 Anticipatory Action pilot in Bentiu, Unity State (funded by a $15 million allocation from OCHA's Central Emergency Response Fund and $4 million from the South Sudan Humanitarian Fund), the primary interventions explicitly targeted WASH and infrastructure.A 2-month INFLOW-AI lead time allows WASH NGOs to physically procure and move high-volume, low-cost supplies (e.g., AquaTabs/chlorine, soap, jerrycans) via river barges or trucks to vulnerable communities before the roads close. Furthermore, it provides the necessary operational window to dispatch engineering teams to mechanically elevate borehole hand-pumps above the projected inundation line and strategically de-sludge high-risk latrines.6.3 Data Science Viability and Ground TruthFrom a modeling perspective, WASH infrastructure is entirely static, making it highly compatible with 252m spatial resolution forecasting. The team can overlay the INFLOW-AI flood probability rasters with OpenStreetMap (OSM) and WASH Cluster datasets that map the precise GPS coordinates of community boreholes, tap stands, and water treatment points.Crucially, the validation crisis is resolved. Rather than relying on fragile DHIS2 clinical delivery data, the data science team can validate their risk model against two robust data streams:WASH Cluster Post-Flood Assessments: Post-disaster surveys explicitly record which specific boreholes were submerged, destroyed, or contaminated, providing a direct physical validation of the model's inundation predictions.IDSR / EWARS Outbreak Alerts: The spatial emergence of cholera and AWD alerts directly correlates with compromised water points, offering an epidemiological proxy for flooded infrastructure.6.4 Formulated Research Objective for the WASH PivotResearch Objective:To develop and validate a spatial risk-prioritization algorithm that predicts the submersion of critical point-source water infrastructure (boreholes and tap stands) in South Sudan, utilizing 252m-resolution INFLOW-AI flood forecasts and OpenStreetMap infrastructure data, to enable the anticipatory deployment of localized WASH interventions (borehole elevation and targeted chlorination) by the ZOA/DRA coalition up to 6 dekads (2 months) prior to peak inundation.By executing this strategic pivot, the capstone team transitions from an operationally flawed and statistically un-evaluable clinical premise into a rigorous, highly actionable spatial data science project. This revised focus directly addresses one of the most lethal secondary impacts of climate change in South Sudan while remaining strictly aligned with both academic standards and the stakeholder's operational mandate.
