How much of South Sudan is cropland, really?

Seven public maps disagree on how much farmland South Sudan has, and ZOA's flood trigger depends on that number. We will build our own rule-based cropland map of the whole country for the 2025 season and compare it with WorldCover, GLAD and the others. In ZOA's areas, the five Aweil counties and Bor South, we check every map against points we label ourselves and estimate the cropland area with a confidence interval. We end with a flooded-cropland check.

Summary

The plan in eight lines

  1. Question: how much cropland does South Sudan have in the 2025 season, and how wrong are the public maps that ZOA's trigger would use? We answer the second part with checked numbers in ZOA's areas.
  2. Main result: the cropland area of ZOA's areas (Aweil counties + Bor South) in hectares with a 95% confidence interval, from about 450 randomly placed points that two of us label by eye. This number does not depend on any map.
  3. Second result: our own cropland map of the whole country for 2025, built from written if-then rules on Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1. The rules come from what the labellers look at. It is checked only in ZOA's areas, and the page says so wherever it is shown.
  4. Comparison: nationally, cropland area per map and per state next to the FAO/WFP estimate (CFSAM). In ZOA's areas, our map and seven public maps (WorldCover, GLAD, Digital Earth Africa, WorldCereal, ASAP, Esri, Dynamic World) scored on the same held-out points.
  5. Flood piece: the 2025 flood extent over each cropland map, nationally and in ZOA's areas, to show how the choice of map changes whether a "flood inundating cropland" trigger would fire.
  6. Reusable: the map is a pipeline. Set a year and an area in a config file, run it, and get a new map. Each new year needs a small check sample.
  7. Why ours might win: it's local, for the most recent complete season, uses ZOA's definition, and handles the local confusions (burned grass, wild grass that greens up with the crops). It might lose, and that is still a result.
  8. Labelling is done by people, about 4–5 hours each. LLMs help with code and drafts, never with labels.
Scope

The whole country, checked in ZOA's areas

Map: all of South Sudan (~64 million ha). Running the rules over the whole country costs little extra in Earth Engine, and it lets us compare national figures with CFSAM, where the 35× gap on the Sprint 2 slides comes from.

Check: ZOA's two areas. The labelled sample, the accuracy scores and the area estimate are done where ZOA works (Stakeholder Q&A 2, slide 3). Outside them our map is not checked, the same limit we point out for the public maps. A rule tuned in Aweil may not fit the wet south or the eastern plains.

Aweil is a town in north-west South Sudan and the capital of Northern Bahr el Ghazal state. In our work "Aweil" means the state's five counties: Aweil North, Aweil East, Aweil South, Aweil West and Aweil Centre, about 3 million ha in total.

The Lol river crosses the area from west to east. Its floodplain is where ZOA works and where fields flood. Most farming is rain-fed smallholder sorghum, with groundnuts, sesame and maize, mostly in small plots near homesteads. There is also an irrigated rice scheme near Aweil town. Cattle move through the area in the dry season.

Bor South (Jonglei, on the White Nile) is ZOA's other area. On the slides it had 0 ha of flooded cropland detected in any season. It gets its own group of sample points (see the sample section).

The problem

The maps don't agree, and nobody has checked them here

ZOA's trigger needs one number: how much farmland is under water. That number comes from laying a public flood map over a public cropland map, the method of Pacetti et al. (2017) that WFP's ADAM tool uses. Its authors warn that remote sensing alone "lacks the level of accuracy necessary in a comprehensive food security assessment".

  • No ground truth. The largest accuracy study (Kerner et al. 2024) covers 8 African countries, none of them South Sudan.
  • Clouds in the flood season. Optical images are blind in July–September, exactly when fields flood.
  • Small, shifting fields. Plots are small, move between years and lie fallow.
  • Numbers without error bars. Hectares are published as if exact.

If the map misses the fields, the trigger stays silent. A field missing from the map has no flooding and no household to target. That is the SLE core of the project: the map decides whose farmland is seen.

Our method

One sample, two uses

Why the sample comes first

Rules need thresholds, and thresholds need labelled points. A map is judged only on points it never saw. And the area estimate from a probability sample is valid with or without a good map. GLAD uses the same idea for its own area figures.

Why rules

Anyone can read why a pixel is crop, so ZOA or a reviewer can check it. It can run for any year, and most maps are single-year or stopped (Copernicus ends in 2019). It can also take radar, which sees through clouds.

Why ours could be better

for
  • Tuned on points in Aweil, not trained elsewhere.
  • The most recent complete season (2025). Most others are 2019–2021; only Esri and Dynamic World have 2025.
  • ZOA's definition: crop actually grown this season.
  • Explicit rules for the local traps: burned grass, wet-season grass, flood-recession plots, homestead gardens.
against
  • Only ~150 points to set the rules.
  • Labels drawn from 10 m imagery are noisy.
  • The big maps use far more features and data.
  • Fairness: our rules are tuned locally and theirs aren't. That is the point of the test, and the report says so.

What we can claim: "better or worse than map X in ZOA's areas in 2025, on our test points", with error bars. Nationally we can only say how much each map, ours included, calls crop, not which is right. Not "a better cropland map" in general. If the rules lose, the area estimate still stands, and "local rules don't beat global models here" is a finding.

Algorithm

From what the eye sees to a rule

In this savanna, crops and wild grass turn green together when the rains come. That is why the maps disagree. What separates them is timing and context: fields are bare and tilled before sowing, they are harvested while the grass stays green, they have regular edges, and they sit near homesteads.

Rule 1 and a benchmark

Rule 1 · our map

Small decision tree

2–4 levels (picked by cross-validation), trained on the calibration points. It is deterministic and prints as if-then rules; the data sets the thresholds. It maps land cropped in 2025. Fallow looks like grass in a single year, so fallow enters the cropland area through the labelled sample, not the map.

benchmark only

Black box

A random forest on the same features, and one on Google's Satellite Embedding. It shows what we give up for readable rules. If it wins by a lot, we report it.

Rule 1 is frozen in a dated file before anyone looks at the test points.

# What a printed Rule 1 could look like (made up; the real one comes from the tree).
not crop  if  permanent_water_share > 0.5
not crop  if  ndvi_dry_season_p10  > 0.45            # trees and evergreen shrubs
not crop  if  burned_nov_feb and not bare_apr_may
crop      if  bare_soil_apr_may   > t1
          and ndvi_rise_jul_aug   > t2
          and harvest_drop_vs_500m > t3
          and dist_to_buildings    < 2000 m           # optional, test with and without
otherwise not crop

Imagery for labelling

What we'll use

  • Sentinel-2 2025, monthly true colour and false colour, and the NDVI curve for each point, in our labelling tool. Good for timing (bare, green, harvested); weak for tiny plots.
  • Google Earth Pro historical imagery (free desktop app). Sub-metre images on some dates, for field shapes.
  • Esri World Imagery Wayback: older versions of Esri's high-resolution basemap.
  • Sentinel-1 for cloudy months.

What we lost, and what to try

  • The free Planet NICFI basemaps (4.8 m, monthly) that Kerner labelled from ended in 2025. Norway cancelled the next phase, and Collect Earth Online no longer shows them.
  • Try Planet's Education & Research programme with a TU/e or Tilburg email. Don't count on it.
  • Ask ZOA for GPS field points or photos, and check WorldCereal's reference data for South Sudan points. Either would be the best data we could get.

Sample

How precise will the area be?

The sample covers ZOA's two areas as separate groups: about 330 points in the Aweil counties and 120 in Bor South, each split by how many maps say crop. The calculator shows one area at a time. The area estimate is a weighted average of the share of crop points in each group, weighted by how much land each group covers. The uncertainty comes almost entirely from the big group where no map sees crop, because a small error there covers a huge area. So most points should go there. Try it below.

Stratified estimator: p̂ = Σ Wh p̂h, SE = √Σ Wh² p̂h(1−p̂h)/(nh−1), 95% CI = ±1.96 SE (Olofsson et al. 2014; Stehman 2014).

With ~330 points in the Aweil counties expect roughly ±2.5 percentage points; Bor South, with ~120, will be wider. That is wide in relative terms, but it's enough to tell whether cropland is nearer 1% (what most maps say) or 5% (what CFSAM models). Plain random sampling would need about 1,800 points for ±1 point.

Labelling protocol, per point

Record

  • Label: crop / not crop / unsure
  • Cues seen: bare soil Apr–Jun · green-up month · early harvest drop · regular plots · homesteads within ~300 m · burn scar · standing water
  • Imagery used and its date; confidence 1–3; time spent

Rules for labellers

  • Never open a cropland map while labelling.
  • Two people per point, independently.
  • Pilot first: 60 points near Turalei (Twic county, outside the study area), then fix the protocol.
  • About 1.5 minutes per point: 450 × 2 ≈ 22 hours, ~4.5 hours each for five people.
Reuse

A map you can rerun next year

Yes, we build it so it runs again. The same code takes a year and an area from a config file and produces a new map from new satellite data. The rules live in one JSON file that both the Python scoring code and the Earth Engine map code read, so the map and the scores can never drift apart.

# cropland/config.yaml
year: 2025
aoi: south_sudan            # map extent
check_areas: [aweil_5_counties, bor_south]
rules: rules_2022-10-11.json
season:
  bare:    [Apr, May]
  greenup: [Jun, Aug]
  harvest: [Sep, Oct]
cloud_score_min: 0.6
export_scale_m: 10
seed: 42

For a new year

  1. Change year, run the pipeline (about an hour in Earth Engine).
  2. Label a small check sample, ~100 points (~3 hours for two people).
  3. If accuracy holds, publish. If it drops, recalibrate the thresholds and state it.

Rules from 2022 will not transfer perfectly: a late rainy season shifts every date. To help, timing features are measured relative to the season's rain onset (CHIRPS) and relative to the surrounding grass, not as fixed NDVI values.

Keep in mind

  • Agreement between maps is not accuracy: maps trained on similar data make the same errors.
  • The CFSAM figure is modelled from household and population statistics. It's context, not the truth.
  • A single season (2025), checked in two areas: say so in every conclusion about the national map.
  • Hectares are not hunger: flooded cropland is one factor among many for IDPs, hosts and pastoralists.
FAQ

Questions you'll probably ask

Why map the whole country but check only ZOA's areas?

Mapping is cheap in Earth Engine, checking is not: every checked zone needs its own ~150 labelled points. We put the labelling where ZOA acts, so those numbers are solid, and we say clearly that the rest of our map is unchecked.

What if our map loses to WorldCover?

We report it. The area estimate doesn't depend on our map, and "local readable rules don't beat global models in Aweil" is a useful finding for anyone choosing a map.

Why not just train a big model?

150 labelled points can't train a big model well, and ZOA can't check a black box. We still run one as a benchmark, to show what readable rules cost in accuracy.

Can an LLM do the labelling?

No. The labels are the ground truth everything is scored against. LLMs write code and drafts; people label.

Is 450 points enough?

For each of ZOA's areas, yes: about ±2 percentage points in the Aweil counties, wider in Bor South. For ranking two similar maps, maybe not. If their error bars overlap, we don't rank them.

Why 2025?

It is the most recent complete season. 2026 is still being harvested and has no flood masks yet. The Sprint 2 slides used 2022; those numbers stay as the motivation. The pipeline can run 2026 once the season closes.

What happened to the flood forecast model?

It's documented and reproducible in prediction/ and MODEL_REPORT.html, but it's not part of this plan. The flood piece uses the observed 2025 flood extent, not a forecast.

Why keep points where labellers disagree?

Those are the hard cases: field edges, fallow, crops mixed into grass. Dropping them, as Kerner et al. did, makes every map look better than it is.

Words used here

NDVIA greenness index from red and near-infrared light; higher means more living vegetation.
Stratified sampleRandom points drawn separately in groups (strata), here by how many maps say crop.
Precision / recallPrecision: of what a map calls crop, how much is crop. Recall: of the real crop, how much the map finds.
Confidence intervalThe range the true area lies in with 95% confidence, given our sample.
Sentinel-1 / -2ESA satellites: radar (sees through clouds) and optical (10 m colour images every ~5 days).
