# Critical Feasibility Assessment of Anticipatory Action for Maternal and Newborn Health in Flood-Affected South Sudan

## Executive Verdict

**Recommendation: NO-GO for the objective as currently framed. Conditional GO only after reframing it from “predicting cut-off deliveries and MNH kit needs” to “predicting flood-induced health-facility and referral-access disruption.”**

The underlying humanitarian problem is real: floods routinely submerge facilities, block patients and staff, interrupt supplies, and force services to relocate. In 2021, UNICEF counted 121 flood-affected health facilities, while WHO reported more than 60 facilities cut off, submerged, or damaged and 18 facilities relocated in Unity State; in 2024, WHO reported 58 submerged facilities alongside increased malaria, snakebite, and outbreak risks. However, these reports describe **system-wide health disruption**, not evidence that MNH supplies are consistently the first or dominant lifeline lost.[^1][^2][^3]

The current proposition has three fundamental weaknesses:

- It assumes supply shortages are the principal causal bottleneck, although evidence from South Sudan points simultaneously to blocked transport, flooded or abandoned facilities, shortages of skilled staff, weak referral systems, insecurity, cost, and poor perceived quality.[^4][^5][^6][^7]
- ZOA’s established sectors are food security and livelihoods, WASH, peacebuilding, education, and shelter—not clinical health or reproductive-health commodity management.[^8][^9][^10]
- A model of “cut-off deliveries” would have no dependable observed outcome against which to validate predictions. DHIS2 reporting is incomplete and becomes least reliable precisely where flooding causes access and communication failures.[^11][^12]

| Criterion | Current MNH objective | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Humanitarian importance | Maternal and newborn emergencies are highly time-sensitive | Strong |
| Evidence that MNH is disrupted by floods | Yes, but usually as part of wider health-system disruption | Moderate |
| Evidence that stock pre-positioning is the principal solution | No; access, staff, referral and facility functionality are equally or more important | Weak |
| Mandate fit for ZOA | Poor without a Health Cluster, UNFPA, UNICEF, or clinical partner | Weak |
| Data availability for exposure modelling | Available but uncertain and frequently outdated | Moderate |
| Ground truth for cut-off deliveries or maternal deaths | Very limited | Very weak |
| Academic evaluability | Weak if output is only population multiplied by demographic constants | Weak |
| Value of a two-month forecast | Potentially high for domestic redistribution, boats, mobile teams, temporary relocation, and route planning | Strong |
| Overall | **NO-GO as framed; conditional GO after access-centred redesign** | **NO-GO** |

A defensible project must treat expected pregnancies and deliveries as **vulnerability overlays**, not as the model’s predicted ground truth. The primary outcome should be an observable operational event such as facility isolation, referral-route failure, temporary facility closure, or inability to complete a scheduled resupply.

## Clinical and Logistical Reality

### What floods actually disrupt

The available evidence does not establish an ordering in which MNH services are “first” to fail. Floods simultaneously affect facilities, roads, WASH, nutrition sites, vaccination, malaria treatment, epidemic surveillance, and supply delivery.

- The 2022 national flood assessment found reports of health facilities being washed away, equipment and buildings submerged, and health workers deserting facilities; seven health units in Upper Nile were reportedly submerged and partially or completely collapsed.[^5]
- In Unity State, reaching some facilities can require seven to eight hours by canoe or three to four hours by motorboat, while flooding of Chotyiel and Rotriak PHCCs impeded patient access, supply delivery, supervision, and emergency referral.[^4]
- In 2024, submerged roads forced the suspension of services at approximately nine UNICEF-supported nutrition sites in parts of Unity State.[^13]
- WHO’s 2024 surveillance reports connected flooding with submerged health facilities, increased malaria morbidity, snakebites, drowning, and concurrent outbreaks.[^3][^14]

Therefore, MNH is a **high-consequence component of an integrated disruption**, not demonstrably the earliest affected service.

### The binding bottlenecks

South Sudan research repeatedly identifies multiple interacting barriers to maternal care:

| Bottleneck | Evidence | Does clinic stock pre-positioning solve it? |
|---|---|---|
| No transport or impassable roads | Long distances, lack of transport, floods, poor roads and washed-out routes reduce antenatal and delivery access.[^7][^15] | No |
| Facility submerged or relocated | Facilities have been washed away, submerged, abandoned or relocated.[^5][^2] | No; stock may also be lost |
| No skilled provider | A South Sudan newborn-care study identified severe shortages of skilled birth attendants as the main bottleneck.[^16] | No |
| Facility stockout | Lack of medicines, supplies and equipment reduces perceived quality and service use.[^6][^15] | Yes, if the facility remains accessible and functional |
| Referral failure | Emergency obstetric care requires transport, communication, surgical capacity and blood transfusion.[^17] | Only partially |
| Cold-chain failure | Oxytocin requires refrigerated transport and storage at 2–8°C.[^18][^19] | Pre-positioning can worsen the problem if refrigeration is unreliable |
| Cost, insecurity and acceptability | Fees, transport cost, discrimination, insecurity and cultural factors contribute to home delivery.[^6][^15] | No |

Under the three-delays framework, stock pre-positioning mainly addresses part of **Delay 3: receiving adequate care after arrival**. It does little about **Delay 2: reaching care**, which flooding directly worsens, and it cannot solve a submerged facility, an absent midwife, a failed referral boat, or an inoperative theatre.

### When pre-positioning helps

Pre-positioning is clinically useful when all of the following are true:

- The receiving facility will remain above water.
- Staff will remain present.
- Women can still reach it, or a functioning community-referral mechanism exists.
- The facility has the skills and infrastructure required for the commodities.
- Cold-chain, security and stock management can be maintained.
- A higher-level referral facility remains accessible for complications.

Clean delivery kits can reduce infection risk where a facility birth is impossible, but they cannot manage obstructed labour, severe haemorrhage requiring surgery or transfusion, eclampsia, uterine rupture, or severe neonatal complications. MISP guidance therefore combines clean-delivery materials with functional basic and comprehensive emergency obstetric and newborn care, referral transport, communication, trained personnel, and supplies.[^20][^17]

UNFPA’s South Sudan flood response reflects this wider package: it supplied reproductive-health kits and clean-delivery kits but also deployed mobile and canoe-based teams, supported facilities, facilitated normal deliveries, and referred or conducted Caesarean sections. This suggests the relevant anticipatory action is not “send more kits,” but **select a context-specific bundle of supplies, staff, transport, referral and temporary service-location actions**.[^21]

### Mandate alignment

ZOA’s published South Sudan work centres on WASH, food security and livelihoods, and peacebuilding. Its broader institutional strategy similarly identifies livelihoods, WASH, peacebuilding, education and shelter as core sectors.[^9][^10][^8]

A DRA consortium can include organizations with health expertise, and standardized IARH kits can be ordered by NGOs working through UNFPA arrangements. Nevertheless, neither DRA membership nor participation in a general humanitarian consortium makes ZOA an appropriate owner of clinical commodity selection, pharmacovigilance, EmONC planning, or reproductive-health procurement.[^22]

An MNH tool would be operationally relevant only if a named health actor agrees to:

- Own the clinical decision.
- Provide facility-functionality, stock, staffing, service and dispatch data.
- Specify feasible anticipatory actions.
- Accept responsibility for clinical and supply-chain validation.
- Use the output in an existing Health Cluster or SRH Working Group process.

Without that commitment, the project would probably produce recommendations for an actor unable to implement them.

## Estimation Methodology

### Select the denominator

Let \(P\) be the estimated population in the area expected to become isolated. It should not simply be the population under predicted water; it should represent people whose **practical route to a functioning service point or referral facility is predicted to fail**.

South Sudan has not had a comprehensive census since 2008, and subsequent estimates depend on projections, surveys and model-based redistribution affected by large-scale displacement. GRID3 offers high-resolution estimates incorporating census projections, building footprints, and IOM and UNHCR displacement information, but these remain modelled estimates rather than observed headcounts.[^23][^24][^25]

At minimum, calculations should be repeated with two population surfaces and reported as an uncertainty interval rather than a single count.

### Estimate live births

If \(b\) is the crude birth rate per 1,000 population per year, expected annual live births are:

\[
B_{\text{year}} = P \times \frac{b}{1000}
\]

For a planning period of \(m\) months:

\[
B_m = P \times \frac{b}{1000} \times \frac{m}{12}
\]

The traditional IARH planning assumption uses a CBR of 4%, equivalent to 40 births per 1,000 population annually. At that rate, expected deliveries over the two-month INFLOW-AI forecast horizon are:[^20]

\[
B_2 = P \times 0.04 \times \frac{2}{12}
\]

\[
B_2 = P \times 0.00667
\]

Thus, a predicted isolated population of 30,000 corresponds to approximately 200 expected live births over two months. That number represents an expected value, not 200 identifiable women or 200 facility deliveries.

### Estimate pregnant women

Two conventions appear in humanitarian guidance and should not be mixed.

**Simplified MISP assumption**

\[
W_{\text{pregnant}} \approx 0.04P
\]

IAWG training materials state that approximately 4% of a crisis-affected population may be pregnant at a given time.[^26]

**CBR-derived method**

The older Inter-Agency Field Manual first estimates annual pregnancies as live births plus approximately 15% for pregnancies ending in miscarriage or stillbirth, then assumes approximately 70% of that annual total are pregnant at a given point:

\[
W_{\text{pregnant}} = B_{\text{year}} \times 1.15 \times 0.70
\]

With \(b=40\):

\[
W_{\text{pregnant}} \approx 0.0322P
\]

The difference between approximately 3.2% and 4% demonstrates why the coefficient should be stated explicitly.

South Sudan planning documents do not consistently use one universal pregnancy multiplier. UNICEF’s 2024 humanitarian planning estimated 832,393 pregnant mothers from a population of 14,864,171, equivalent to 5.6%, but this planning figure should not automatically be interpreted as point prevalence or substituted into IARH calculations. The project should obtain the coefficient used by the current South Sudan SRH Working Group and document whether it represents pregnancies at a point in time, pregnancies during a year, or programme beneficiaries.[^27][^28]

### Estimate complications

Expected potentially life-threatening obstetric complications are conventionally estimated as 15% of expected deliveries:

\[
C_{\text{obstetric}} = 0.15B_m
\]

IAWG and WHO guidance consistently use approximately 15%, while noting that approximately 5% to 15% of deliveries may require Caesarean section and 9% to 15% of newborns may require lifesaving emergency care.[^17][^26]

For \(P=30{,}000\), \(b=40\), and a two-month horizon:

\[
B_2 = 200
\]

\[
C_{\text{obstetric}} = 30
\]

The project must not label those 30 cases as preventable deaths or even observed complications. They are expected clinical workloads derived from a generic coefficient.

### Include forecast uncertainty

A better expected-value calculation combines population, demographic need, and the probability of isolation. For raster cells \(i\):

\[
E(B_{\text{cutoff}}) = \sum_i P_i \times \frac{b_i}{1000} \times \frac{m}{12} \times \Pr(I_i=1)
\]

Here, \(\Pr(I_i=1)\) is the estimated probability that cell \(i\) loses practical access to the relevant service during the forecast horizon. This is preferable to classifying every cell intersecting the binary flood mask as definitely isolated.

The calculation should also distinguish:

- Loss of access to any primary facility.
- Loss of access to a delivery-capable facility.
- Loss of access to BEmONC.
- Loss of referral access to CEmONC.
- Isolation of the patient population versus isolation of the facility from resupply.

These represent different operational and clinical outcomes.

## IARH Kit Translation

### Standard kit levels

IARH kits are designed for specific service levels and generally for a three-month period: community kits for 10,000 people, primary/BEmONC kits for 30,000, and referral/CEmONC kits for approximately 150,000. They are emergency packages, not intended as a permanent resupply system, and local consumption and facility capacity should replace global assumptions as soon as possible.[^29][^30]

| Kit | Function | Standard planning basis | Historical packaging figures |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 2A | Individual clean-delivery packages | 10,000 people for three months; older manual contains 200 individual packages, covering expected births and visibly pregnant women | 92 kg; 0.316 cubic metres |
| 2B | Equipment for birth attendants | 10,000 people for three months; five attendant sets in the older standard | 10.25 kg; 0.038 cubic metres |
| 6A | Reusable clinical-delivery equipment | One BEmONC-level package for 30,000 people over three months | 31 kg; 0.236 cubic metres |
| 6B | Drugs and disposable delivery equipment | Complements 6A; old calculation assumes 300 deliveries per 30,000 people over three months | 101.5 kg; 0.335 cubic metres |
| 8 | Management of miscarriage and abortion complications | 30,000 people over three months; older calculation covers approximately 60 cases | 38 kg; 0.174 cubic metres |
| 11A | Reusable referral-level obstetric equipment | CEmONC referral service covering approximately 150,000 people | 20 kg; 0.086 cubic metres |
| 11B | Drugs and disposable referral equipment | Old calculation covers 1,500 births, 75 Caesarean sections and approximately 30 additional emergencies | 1,083 kg; 3.13 cubic metres |

These physical figures come from the 2011 manual and must be verified against the current kit version and shipment configuration before operational use. In particular, Kit 11B is over one tonne and is not a plausible item for opportunistic forward placement at a small, flood-prone PHCU.[^20]

### Basic quantity formulas

For a three-month period, a preliminary population-based calculation is:

\[
N_2 = \left\lceil \frac{P}{10{,}000} \right\rceil
\]

\[
N_6 = \left\lceil \frac{P}{30{,}000} \right\rceil
\]

\[
N_{11} = \left\lceil \frac{P}{150{,}000} \right\rceil
\]

These are only first-pass screening calculations. Before ordering:

- Kit 2 should be adjusted for the number of visibly pregnant women, expected home births, community distribution capacity, and existing stock.
- Kits 6A and 6B should be sent only to facilities with appropriately trained staff and BEmONC capability.
- Kits 11A and 11B require an operational referral hospital with surgical, anaesthetic, sterilization, blood, power, water and staffing capacity.
- Kit 8 requires trained staff and appropriate sterilization arrangements.
- Reusable parts should not be repeatedly ordered merely because consumables have been depleted.

IAWG explicitly requires assessment of population size, facility type, provider skills, storage and distribution before an order is placed.[^30]

### Costs and shelf life

There is no stable, universally valid “cost per kit.” UNFPA states that prices change periodically and organizations should obtain current prices or a pro forma invoice through Procurement Services. A budget must include kit price, international freight, import and customs handling, Juba storage, onward transport, possible air or river movement, elevated storage, security, cold-chain equipment, wastage and reverse logistics.[^31][^20]

Similarly, an entire kit does not have one meaningful shelf life. It contains reusable equipment and multiple medicines or consumables with product- and batch-specific expiry dates; expiry must therefore be obtained from the current packing list and lot data.

### Cold-chain constraints

WHO recommends that quality-assured oxytocin be protected from light and continuously transported and stored at 2–8°C. Kits 6B, 8 and 11B historically include keep-cool components because they contain oxytocin or other temperature-sensitive items.[^18][^19][^20]

Alternatives have important limitations:

- **Misoprostol:** no refrigeration, but it must be protected from humidity in appropriate packaging; it can support PPH prevention where injectable uterotonics or skilled personnel are unavailable.[^32][^18]
- **Heat-stable carbetocin:** no cold chain and non-inferior to oxytocin for PPH prevention, but it is indicated for prevention rather than treatment and still requires an appropriately trained provider to administer it.[^19][^33]
- **Oxytocin:** remains the preferred uterotonic where quality, cold chain and skilled administration can be assured.[^19]

Consequently, placing oxytocin at an unpowered, flood-prone PHCU without temperature monitoring may create the appearance of preparedness while leaving the medicine ineffective. Misoprostol or other alternatives cannot be selected by a data science model without national-protocol and clinical-governance input.

## Pipeline and Timing

### Existing mechanisms

WHO and UNFPA already pre-position standardized emergency commodities. During the 2021 floods, WHO reported pre-positioning hundreds of Interagency Emergency Health, cholera, pneumonia and nutrition kits at hub offices, with further stocks under preparation. In 2024, WHO again pre-positioned emergency health kits, antimalarials and antivenom, while the SSHF funded integrated health, WASH and shelter action including static and mobile clinics and emergency-health commodities.[^2][^34][^35]

This creates a significant duplication risk. A new model should not estimate how many kits theoretically correspond to a flooded population if UNFPA and WHO already maintain stock, partner pipelines, target lists and flood contingency plans.

The publicly available evidence reviewed here does **not** establish a universal South Sudan MNH procurement calendar running exactly from December to April. That proposition must be tested through interviews with UNFPA, WHO, the Health Cluster, Logistics Cluster, Ministry of Health, and implementing partners rather than treated as an established fact.

### Is two months enough?

A two-month forecast is generally too short for ordinary international procurement. IAWG guidance gives an expected delivery time of approximately 10–12 weeks for post-acute orders after financing is finalized; exceptionally rapid dispatch applies only where emergency stock, payment, import documentation, customs clearance, receiving storage and onward transport are already arranged.[^30]

A two-month lead can still add value for:

- Reallocating stock already held in Juba or state hubs.
- Moving stock from a county warehouse to elevated forward storage.
- Hiring or repositioning boats.
- Pre-negotiating fuel and transporter contracts.
- Temporarily relocating a facility.
- Deploying mobile teams and community midwives.
- Providing individual kits to identified late-trimester women.
- Establishing referral communication and transport.
- Reinforcing solar refrigeration, WASH and secure storage.
- Scheduling earlier routine resupply before roads become impassable.

Therefore, INFLOW-AI should be evaluated as a **last-mile positioning and service-continuity trigger**, not primarily as an international procurement trigger.

## Data Feasibility

### Available inputs

| Data need | Candidate source | Principal limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Flood forecast | INFLOW-AI at 252 m per dekad | Inundation is not equivalent to route impassability or isolation |
| Facility coordinates | MoH lists, WHO dashboard, HDX, OSM/Healthsites | Status, services and staffing change rapidly |
| Facility functionality | WHO/MoH Health Service Functionality Dashboard | Requires access to underlying update dates and definitions |
| Population | GRID3, WorldPop, HRP population tables | Modelled from old census and uncertain displacement patterns |
| Displacement | IOM DTM and UNHCR | Temporal mismatch and incomplete small-area coverage |
| Roads and waterways | OSM and humanitarian common operational datasets | Road existence does not establish wet-season passability |
| Accessibility | Global friction surfaces or locally built cost surfaces | Generic travel speeds poorly represent canoes, seasonal tracks, dykes and informal crossings |
| Service utilization | DHIS2 and partner records | Incomplete, delayed and non-randomly missing |
| Operational access | Health Cluster, Logistics Cluster and partner dispatch logs | Often unstructured, inaccessible or inconsistently archived |
| Commodity stocks | UNFPA, WHO and implementing-partner LMIS records | Usually not public and may use incompatible item definitions |

The WHO/MoH functionality dashboard is a stronger starting point than raw OSM because it combines facility functionality, infrastructure, population, surveillance and service-utilization data. Nevertheless, the official spatial facility inventory itself has historically been inconsistent: one published 2017 compilation listed 1,747 facilities with 13 missing coordinates, while later sources reported substantial non-functionality.[^36][^37]

OSM provides a more recent downloadable facility layer, but volunteered facility data can be incomplete or inconsistent and usually cannot establish whether a maternity ward, theatre, midwife or cold chain is operational. A mapped point should never automatically be interpreted as an active BEmONC or CEmONC facility.[^38][^39]

### Ghost facilities

“Ghost facility” is not a consistently measured official category. Available assessments instead report facilities that are registered but non-functional, partly functional, inaccessible, unstaffed, or unable to provide expected services.

Reported estimates differ sharply by date and denominator:

- A 2018-era facility list cited 200 of 1,483 PHCUs and PHCCs as non-functional, alongside several non-functional county hospitals.[^40]
- The 2020 HNO reported more than 1,300 of approximately 2,300 facilities as non-functional.[^41]
- Another national assessment reported 517 of 1,956 facilities as non-functional.[^42]

These figures should not be averaged. Their disagreement demonstrates that facility status is a time-dependent input requiring direct verification.

### The 252 m problem

A 252 m inundation grid is useful for broad flood exposure, but practical access can depend on features far smaller than one pixel:

- A bridge or culvert.
- A raised road segment.
- A dyke.
- A boat landing.
- A narrow channel.
- A passable footpath.
- Water depth and velocity.
- Availability of a canoe, motorboat or fuel.

Consequently, the team should not infer isolation from simple intersection of a route with a predicted flooded pixel. Accessibility should be represented probabilistically and calibrated using observed route or dispatch failures.

## Validation Problem

### Why DHIS2 is insufficient

South Sudan’s health-information system remains too incomplete to serve as the sole ground truth. A WHO assessment reported 62% completeness for routine reports and 57% completeness for indicator data. A more recent World Bank implementation review raised concerns about facility reporting completeness, discrepancies in maternal and newborn data, uncertain population denominators, and the absence of third-party verification.[^12][^11]

Flood-related non-reporting is especially problematic because missing data are **not missing at random**. A facility may report zero deliveries because it was closed, inaccessible, without staff, unable to transmit data, or because women delivered elsewhere; DHIS2 alone cannot distinguish those mechanisms.

Maternal deaths are an even less suitable label because:

- Many deaths occur outside facilities.
- Civil registration is incomplete.
- Events are rare at facility-dekad scale.
- Reporting failure is correlated with flooding.
- A maternal death cannot generally be attributed to lack of stock rather than transport, referral, staffing or clinical quality.

### Validatable outcomes

A rigorous project should use a hierarchy of observable outcomes:

| Outcome | Possible evidence | Suitability |
|---|---|---|
| Predicted route becomes impassable | Partner access log, Logistics Cluster alert, geotagged field verification | Strong |
| Facility inaccessible by normal transport | Facility phone survey or partner report | Strong |
| Scheduled resupply fails or changes transport mode | Waybill and dispatch records | Strong |
| Facility relocates or suspends services | WHO/MoH functionality update | Strong |
| Mobile team or boat deployment required | Partner operational record | Strong |
| Facility stops reporting | DHIS2 | Weak alone; useful only as a secondary signal |
| Decline in facility deliveries | DHIS2 plus reporting and relocation controls | Moderate to weak |
| Maternal or neonatal death | Facility and community surveillance | Very weak as a model-development label |
| Estimated cut-off births | Demographic calculation | Not an observed outcome |

The primary unit should be **facility-dekad**, not individual woman or raster cell. Each label should distinguish flood-caused disruption from insecurity, staffing, funding, conflict, equipment failure and other causes.

### Academic evaluation design

A defensible retrospective evaluation would:

1. Select two or three repeatedly flooded counties in Unity, Jonglei or Upper Nile.
2. Verify the active facility list and referral hierarchy for each historical flood season.
3. Construct facility-dekad access labels from 2020–2024 partner, functionality, relocation, route and dispatch records.
4. Train on earlier seasons and test on a completely held-out flood season.
5. Compare against a static seasonal-climatology baseline and a simple observed-inundation baseline.
6. Evaluate different forecast leads from one to six dekads.
7. Run sensitivity analyses using alternative population surfaces, facility-status assumptions and travel thresholds.
8. Conduct a prospective “shadow-mode” season in which predictions are recorded before outcomes but do not automatically trigger clinical action.

Appropriate metrics include:

- Precision-recall area under the curve for rare disruptions.
- Recall among the top \(K\) facilities that available resources could support.
- Brier score and reliability plots for probability calibration.
- Lead-time-specific recall.
- False-alert burden.
- Percentage of actual disruptions captured by the ranked priority list.
- Incremental value over the existing annual pre-positioning plan.

Accuracy or F-score alone would be inadequate because operational capacity is constrained and the cost of missing a critical referral facility differs from the cost of falsely flagging a small health post.

## Fatal Flaws

### No operational owner

Without UNFPA, the Health Cluster, Ministry of Health, UNICEF, WHO, or a clinical NGO as the decision owner, the team cannot determine which facilities can use which kits, obtain stock data, validate clinical assumptions, or act on recommendations. ZOA alone is not an adequate clinical-product owner given its published sector focus.[^10][^8]

### Wrong causal target

The initial hypothesis treats a commodity shortage as the mechanism between flooding and mortality. South Sudan evidence shows that transport, distance, flooding, facility functionality, staffing, perceived quality, cost, security and supplies all contribute. A supply-only model may optimize the wrong intervention.[^6][^7][^15][^16]

### Circular, unvalidated output

A calculation of population multiplied by 4%, then by 15%, and converted into kits does not learn from data. It reproduces international planning constants and cannot demonstrate predictive validity unless it is linked to observed access disruption, demand, stock consumption or service outcomes.

### Non-random missing outcomes

DHIS2 reporting failure may itself be an effect of the flood. Treating missing reports as zero deliveries or facility closure would systematically bias the model toward precisely the places where the outcome is least observed.[^11][^12]

### Dynamic facilities and population

Facility status, staffing and service capability can change faster than static lists are updated, while displacement makes small-area population estimates uncertain. A high-resolution raster does not compensate for uncertain denominators.[^24][^41][^23]

### Forecast-action mismatch

A two-month lead is useful for domestic logistics but frequently shorter than ordinary IARH procurement lead times. If the intended action requires international procurement, customs clearance or construction, a technically accurate prediction may arrive too late.[^30]

### Storage and cold chain

A flood-prone PHCU may be the worst place to store a large quantity of commodities. Flood assessments document submersion, washed-away facilities, equipment loss and staff departure, while oxytocin requires continuous cold-chain management.[^18][^5]

### Ethical and governance risk

A priority score can appear more clinically authoritative than its inputs justify. False precision could divert scarce supplies from functioning facilities, deprioritize populations missing from the raster, or recommend advanced kits to sites unable to use them safely.

## Rigorous Objective

### Recommended formulation

> **Develop and retrospectively evaluate a probabilistic decision-support model that predicts, at facility-dekad level and up to six dekads in advance, flood-induced loss of practical access between verified functional primary health facilities, their catchment populations, and designated BEmONC or CEmONC referral facilities in selected flood-prone counties of South Sudan. The model will combine INFLOW-AI inundation forecasts, verified facility functionality and service capability, road and waterway networks, population and displacement estimates, and historical partner access or resupply records. Its performance will be compared with static seasonal-risk and current-planning baselines using precision-recall performance, probability calibration, recall among the top operationally supportable facilities, and useful lead time. Outputs will support Health Cluster or UNFPA decisions on early resupply, temporary relocation, boat-based referral, mobile-team deployment and elevated storage; estimates of expected pregnancies and deliveries will be presented only as secondary vulnerability indicators.**

### Academic criteria

| Criterion | Specification |
|---|---|
| Action | Develop and retrospectively evaluate a probabilistic facility-access disruption model |
| Outcome | Observed loss of access, failed resupply, relocation, closure, or transport-mode change |
| Scope | Two or three named counties; facility-dekad unit; 2020–2024 retrospective seasons |
| Inputs | INFLOW-AI, verified facilities, routes, waterways, population, displacement, access and dispatch records |
| Baselines | Static flood climatology; previous-dekad inundation; existing annual priority list |
| Primary metric | Precision-recall area under the curve and recall among top \(K\) facilities |
| Secondary metrics | Brier score, calibration, false-alert burden and useful lead time |
| Stakeholder purpose | Rank feasible early actions by Health Cluster, UNFPA or a clinical implementing partner |
| MNH role | Vulnerability weighting and expected workload, not the prediction target |
| Exclusions | No prediction of maternal deaths; no autonomous clinical or procurement decisions |

### Mandatory GO gates

Proceed only if all five conditions are satisfied:

- A named health stakeholder signs off on the research question and intended action.
- Historical facility-level access or dispatch labels are obtainable for at least two flood seasons.
- Facility functionality and EmONC capability can be field-verified.
- At least one action can realistically be implemented within a two-month lead.
- The evaluation can compare against the stakeholder’s current prioritization process.

Failure of either of the first two gates should terminate the MNH-focused project.

## Strongest Alternative Pivot

### Integrated health-access continuity

The strongest alternative is:

> **Predict which verified functional health facilities and referral routes will become inaccessible within one to six dekads, and rank feasible last-mile continuity actions for integrated emergency health services.**

This problem is more defensible because South Sudan flood responses already track submerged facilities, access failures, mobile clinics, emergency-health-kit distributions, antimalarials, antivenom, cholera supplies and relocations. It also avoids pretending that MNH is the sole or first disrupted service when malaria, diarrhoea, malnutrition, cholera, snakebite, vaccination and reproductive care are disrupted together.[^43][^44][^34][^35][^2][^3]

The model’s output could rank actions such as:

- Early routine resupply.
- Elevated or waterproof storage.
- Facility relocation.
- Motorboat or canoe support.
- Alternative referral routes.
- Mobile integrated health team deployment.
- Temporary solar refrigeration.
- WASH reinforcement at health facilities.
- Community distribution of selected low-risk commodities.
- Priority communication checks with isolated facilities.

MNH remains operationally important through higher weights for delivery-capable and referral facilities, expected pregnancies, and time-critical obstetric referral. But the research target becomes something observable and evaluable: **whether access or resupply failed, when it failed, and whether the forecast improved action lead time over current practice.**

If the only committed stakeholder is ZOA, the scope should narrow further to **flood risk to access, WASH, storage and continuity infrastructure at health facilities**, leaving clinical commodities and service prioritization to a Health Cluster partner. This retains the value of INFLOW-AI while aligning the project with ZOA’s demonstrated competencies.[^8][^10]
