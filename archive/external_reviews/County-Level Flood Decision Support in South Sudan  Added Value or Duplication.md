# County-Level Flood Decision Support in South Sudan: Added Value or Duplication?

## Executive judgment

A county-level, exposure-coupled flood ranking could be useful **only if it is redesigned as a decision-specific, uncertainty-aware logistics tool and tested against simple and operational baselines**. As currently framed—combine satellite inundation or a seasonal hazard outlook with WorldPop, ASAP cropland and livelihood zones, then rank counties—it substantially duplicates capabilities already available to South Sudan’s humanitarian system. WFP’s ADAM reports already aggregate observed flood area, exposed population and flooded cropland at administrative level 2, including county tables; Flood-PROOFS East Africa already couples forecast inundation with population, cropland, grazing land, livestock, roads and other exposure classes; REACH has produced multi-year flood-frequency and exposure analyses; and OCHA/cluster processes already turn forecasts, needs, capacity and consultations into prioritized counties.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

The strongest disconfirming evidence is newer than much of the original anticipatory-action literature. A South Sudan Red Cross simplified Early Action Protocol approved in October 2025 now uses the INFLOW-AI model to forecast Sudd inundation two months ahead, with upstream lake levels, antecedent inundation, rainfall, soil moisture and teleconnection indices; it is tied to an explicit trigger, prepositioning budget and predefined actions. The forecasts are public and shared through the official Flood Taskforce technical working group. Thus, the proposed project’s hazard concept is no longer clearly novel in 2026.[^5][^6]

There remains a real research gap, but it is not “another national county ranking.” The defensible contribution is to determine **whether forecast-conditioned decisions improve on chronic-risk maps and current coordination products for a named NGO with a fixed footprint**, then design a constrained allocation or logistics decision aid around that result. The primary outcomes should be avoided stock-outs, people reachable before road closure, tonnes delivered by surface transport, cost per person served and regret under false alarms—not only agreement with a satellite flood mask.

### Separate exposure categories

No single reviewed product perfectly matches the proposed package of **seasonal lead + county aggregation + separate population, cropland and livestock/rangeland + NGO priority ranking**. That narrow combination is a gap, but most of its components already exist:

- WFP ADAM provides admin-level-2 observed flood exposure for population and cropland, including county breakdowns.[^3]
- Flood-PROOFS includes population, cropland, grazing land and livestock units separately, but aggregates impact to first-level administrative regions and has only a five-day forecast range.[^4]
- FAO post-event assessments have estimated county or state cropland damage and livestock affected/dead; for 2021, FAO assessed 45 counties across eight states and estimated both cereal damage and livestock mortality.[^20]
- REACH and World Bank products combine multi-year inundation with population or infrastructure exposure and use the results to identify flood-affected counties.[^2][^21]

Consequently, a new tool cannot claim novelty merely from spatial intersection. Plausible added information would be: (1) a calibrated probability distribution for county exposure two months ahead; (2) uncertainty from alternative flood and population datasets; (3) dynamic displacement, market and road-access layers; (4) NGO warehouses, partners, sector mandate and transport constraints; and (5) a transparent estimate of the **marginal value of acting on the forecast rather than normal prepositioning**.

## The real operational bottleneck

The evidence does not support treating analytical prioritization as the dominant system constraint. South Sudan response plans and evaluations repeatedly identify underfunding, physical and negotiated access, insecurity, bureaucratic impediments, high transport costs, warehousing gaps, import delays and weak government engagement. The 2022 HRP noted declining funding, inaccessible routes, illegal taxation, extortion and places in Jonglei and Unity reachable only by air or river.[^22]

Independent evaluations reach the same conclusion. The Dutch humanitarian-assistance case study found the main impediments were security, access and lack of government engagement, while national NGOs also lacked wet-season logistics capacity. WFP’s country-strategy evaluation found targeting difficult because of the scale of need and funding shortfalls; access, flooding and warehouse gaps affected timeliness even though common logistics services had high user satisfaction. An Australian Humanitarian Partnership evaluation identified security, geography, infrastructure, seasonality and logistics—not missing county scores—as the major deviations from plan.[^23][^24][^25]

This does not make analysis irrelevant. Better prioritization can matter at the margin when scarce stocks must be distributed among feasible hubs. But the tool should represent binding constraints explicitly: funds available by month, procurement lead times, storage capacity, surface corridors, partner presence, access permissions, security and the cost of reallocating staff. Otherwise it ranks places that an NGO cannot reach, is not mandated to serve or lacks resources to support.

## How NGOs choose locations

### Evidence from ZOA, Dorcas and DRA

Public programme records show substantial path dependence. ZOA-Dorcas maintained offices and programmes in Western Bahr el Ghazal, Warrap, Jonglei and Greater Pibor, with field offices in Wau, Akon North, Bor and Pibor. Its women’s empowerment project, for example, operated from January 2021 to July 2023 in specified payams of Wau and Gogrial West rather than reallocating annually according to flood rank.[^26][^27]

The Dutch Relief Alliance’s South Sudan Joint Response also uses multi-year phases and stable partnerships. The 2024–2026 phase continued in Malakal, Melut, Tonj North and Pibor while adding Abyei, Akobo, Mayendit and Rubkona; county assignments were linked to named DRA and national partners. Earlier joint responses repeatedly included locations such as Aweil East, Wau, Pibor, Malakal and Fashoda, consistent with continuing footprints and partnerships.[^28][^29][^30]

This evidence indicates that geographic selection combines:

- Severity and vulnerability evidence, including HNO/IPC and community priorities.
- Existing field offices, local partners and staff.
- Donor-approved proposals, sector mandates and multi-year commitments.
- Cluster coordination and avoidance of duplication.
- Security, physical access, warehouses and feasible delivery modalities.
- Political and government acceptance.

A World Bank/IOM flood project makes the trade-off explicit: counties were shortlisted using flood affectedness, but final choice also required IOM presence, local political support, access, security and absorptive capacity. This is a better model of actual targeting than a hazard-exposure rank alone.[^31]

### Can portfolios change?

They can, but not frictionlessly. The DRA record shows both continuity and additions between funding phases, so portfolios are neither permanently fixed nor re-ranked from zero every year. Emergency top-ups, rapid-response mechanisms and local partner networks allow some movement, while buildings, contracts, relationships, donor budgets and multi-year outcomes create switching costs.[^28]

No reviewed public document demonstrated ZOA, Dorcas or a comparable INGO moving its whole South Sudan county portfolio year-to-year **because of a seasonal flood forecast**. The strongest documented examples of forecast-responsive geographic action instead come from system-level or movement actors: the 2024 national Flood Plan prioritized 20 counties, WHO reports using a flood-projection dashboard for mobile-clinic deployment and logistics, and the South Sudan Red Cross now links a two-month trigger to early action. Absence of public evidence is not proof that INGOs never adapt internally, but it makes the project’s assumed decision process unverified.[^32][^6][^1]

## Hazard predictability

### What was known before INFLOW

OCHA’s attempt to establish a formal South Sudan flood anticipatory-action framework in 2022 concluded that the Sudd’s complex hydrology made the available forecasts insufficient for a reliable time-bound trigger. Rainfall forecasts, local rainfall, hydrological models and upstream levels were all plausible signals, but observed trends did not show a clear enough relationship to predict the timing of extreme inundation. OCHA therefore used “early action” based on standing water and pre-existing vulnerability rather than a formal forecast trigger.[^33][^34]

Flood-PROOFS provides useful five-day regional guidance, not the requested two-to-three-month county forecast. Its validation across 78 Greater Horn gauges had a median Kling–Gupta efficiency of -0.76 and median correlation of 0.35; poor correlations occurred below Lakes Victoria, Kyoga and Albert where release rules are difficult to predict. The authors could not robustly verify extreme-threshold performance because most gauge records were short.[^4]

Seasonal rainfall outlooks do not close this gap. East African precipitation skill depends strongly on season, region and lead; ECMWF studies find JJA performance weaker than other seasons in parts of equatorial East Africa, while longer-range skill drops and rainfall biases remain. Even a skillful seasonal rainfall category does not uniquely determine Sudd extent because storage, evapotranspiration, managed releases, upstream routing and antecedent inundation intervene.[^35][^36]

### Newer evidence

The position changed materially in 2025–2026. INFLOW-AI now predicts South Sudan flood extent six dekads ahead using antecedent inundation and hydroclimatic predictors, and it has been incorporated into the South Sudan Red Cross early-action protocol and official Flood Taskforce information flows. A 2026 preprint reports that, against a raw-inundation persistence model, the transformer reduced hold-out test mean absolute error by 50% and mean squared error by 78%; gains for out-of-sample extremes were smaller—19% and 29%, respectively.[^6][^5]

These results support **predictability of aggregate flood extent**, but they do not yet validate the proposed claim of county-level exposed population, crops or livestock at two-to-three-month lead. The operational protocol’s trigger concerns a greater than 5% change in national inundated area from the seasonal minimum, not a county impact ranking. A new project should therefore avoid presenting upstream lake levels or seasonal rainfall as already verified county predictors.[^6]

A further warning comes from WFP’s 2025 monitor: Lake Victoria’s 2022 peak was the lowest of 2020–2024 but preceded the record flood extent, while similar 2023 lake levels preceded the lowest extent of that period. WFP explicitly states that Lake Victoria peak levels do not directly map to same-year South Sudan flood extent. New hydrological work points to long memory and an approximately 16-month lag between upstream lake maxima and wetland response, which is inconsistent with a simple May lake-level-to-September county ranking.[^13][^37]

### Persistence versus variation

Flood exposure is strongly persistent but not static. FAO described persistent flooding in Unity since 2019, REACH identified Unity, Warrap, Jonglei and Upper Nile as repeatedly affected from 2019–2022, and the 2025 WFP monitor reported the highest seasonal-minimum Sudd extent on record. The South Sudan Red Cross protocol itself selects broad high-risk geographies using historical exposure and notes that some Sudd areas remain flooded year-round.[^38][^39][^13][^6]

Nevertheless, a static map is not guaranteed to be equivalent. The 2020 response covered 36 counties, the September 2024 snapshot reported 42 counties and Abyei, and the timing and source of inundation differ among Nile overflow, tributary flooding and local rainfall. REACH also found that national remote sensing may underestimate short-lived flooding in vegetated parts of Jur River County.[^40][^41][^17][^38]

The decisive analysis has not been published: compare the forecast against simple baselines for each year, including last year’s flood mask, long-run flood frequency, current June inundation and a fixed list of historically exposed counties. Report top-k overlap, rank correlation, Brier or continuous ranked probability scores and decision value. If the same ten to fifteen counties dominate and the forecast does not improve allocation outcomes, the predictive layer adds little. If it identifies unusual expansion, changed access corridors or exposed settlements within a fixed portfolio, it can still be valuable even when the county list changes little.

## Evaluation validity

### Satellite exposure is not impact ground truth

Intersecting an observed satellite flood mask with population or cropland can legitimately evaluate a model’s **predicted exposure under that same exposure definition**. It does not validate people affected, crop loss, livestock mortality, displacement, unmet need or operational benefit. Rapid satellite inundation products should not be treated as absolute truth because cloud, vegetation, acquisition timing, water classification and algorithm choices create errors.[^42][^43]

The design becomes circular when the predictor and target share the same processing chain or closely related satellite signals—for example, antecedent WFP/MODIS inundation predicting a future WFP/MODIS inundation label. The model may learn sensor seasonality, gap filling or classification persistence rather than humanitarian impact. This is not invalid forecasting, but performance must be described as **agreement with an Earth-observation product**, not independent impact accuracy.

A defensible design would separate three tests:

1. **Hazard verification:** compare predicted extent with an independent sensor or method where possible, such as Sentinel-1 SAR against an optical/thermal target, and include uncertainty masks.
2. **Impact verification:** compare predicted exposed people and assets with displacement, assessed affected people, crop/livestock losses and facility disruption, acknowledging that these measure different outcomes.
3. **Decision evaluation:** test whether the forecast would have changed feasible prepositioning early enough to reduce costs, delays or unmet needs.

### Available independent observations

| Dataset | What it can validate | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| IOM DTM Event Tracking | New displacement events, approximate timing and locations | Ad hoc rather than systematic; generally tracks events over 50 households; coverage and access are incomplete.[^44][^45] |
| OCHA affected-person dashboards and flood updates | County affected and sometimes displaced totals | Definitions combine diverse consequences; verification depends on partners and local authorities. In 2022, 1.84 million were estimated affected but only 1.09 million verified.[^46] |
| REACH assessments | Local impacts, service access, community reports and detailed county context | Selected accessible/project locations; qualitative or non-probability methods often limit national comparability.[^17][^47] |
| FAO/WFP CFSAM and flood-impact assessments | Cultivated area damaged, production loss, livestock affected or dead | Episodic, sector-specific and partly reliant on estimates; coverage and methods vary by year.[^20][^48] |
| HNO/ISNA/FSNMS | Multi-sector needs and county severity | Not a pure flood outcome; access barriers and changing questionnaires reduce longitudinal consistency.[^49] |
| Infrastructure/service assessments | Flooded buildings, roads, health, education and water points | Often only available for selected counties and years; facility registries may be incomplete.[^50] |

Reporting bias is serious but not fatal. DTM and displacement estimates explicitly acknowledge undercounting caused by insecurity, access limits and omission of small sites. ISNA teams also failed to reach some planned locations because of bureaucratic barriers. As a result, a naive model can appear to “overpredict” precisely where observation is weakest, or learn that accessible places have more impacts.[^49][^51][^52]

Evaluation should model the observation process: retain an assessment-coverage indicator, distinguish zero from unassessed, use bounds rather than point estimates, stratify by access, and conduct sensitivity analysis that imputes plausible missed events. Claims of humanitarian impact skill should be withheld if conclusions reverse under reasonable under-reporting assumptions.

## Logistics and costs

### Is the premium real?

The direction is strongly supported: surface prepositioning before the rains is far cheaper than emergency air delivery. But “six-to-seven times” is not a stable South Sudan parameter.

- UNICEF’s 2017–2018 analysis estimated air movement at five times road cost and reported about US$12 million saved through dry-season road prepositioning.[^53]
- A World Bank/WFP comparison reported 85% savings for road and river movements relative to hypothetical air transport, equivalent to air costing roughly 6.5 times as much in that comparison.[^54]
- WFP’s 2024 public statement said airdrops could cost as much as 17 times road transport and attributed US$30 million in added costs to late arrival of food.[^55]
- An older Logistics Cluster route example to Rumbek found combined road-air transport about three times direct road transport.[^56]
- WFP’s 2023 report attributed US$43 million in savings and an 18% overall supply-chain-cost reduction to early prepositioning, multimodal transport and road investment.[^57]

These figures are not interchangeable. Some compare airdrops, some landed airlift, some hypothetical substitution, and some total system savings that include route optimization or infrastructure. Rates vary by aircraft, payload, backhaul, origin, route, road condition, warehousing and security. A project should use route- and commodity-specific Logistics Cluster or NGO invoices, not a universal multiplier.

### Does the saving accrue to a small NGO?

Potentially, but not automatically. Small NGOs often procure common logistics or UNHAS/cluster services rather than chartering equivalent flights. Their realized benefit depends on whether earlier analysis changes a shipment that they control, whether stock and funding arrive in time, whether secure storage exists, and whether donor rules permit movement or contingency inventory. WFP-scale aggregate savings cannot simply be assigned to ZOA or a DRA partner.

The relevant counterfactual is also not “forecast versus no prepositioning.” Prepositioning is established standard practice: WFP historically positioned a large share of annual requirements in the first quarter; its 2023 operation positioned 97% of requirements by June after early funding; and humanitarian plans routinely call for prepositioning food, seeds, livestock inputs and WASH supplies. The proper comparison is **routine chronic-risk prepositioning versus forecast-conditioned adjustments**.[^58][^18][^57]

If the forecast merely confirms that Unity, Jonglei and Upper Nile will flood, it may not change logistics. Value is more likely where it changes quantities among existing hubs, identifies an unusual corridor closure, advances procurement, protects stocks, or triggers cash and community action. Funding timing may dominate: WFP’s 2023 success was enabled by carry-over resources and early confirmation of funding.[^57]

## Data-quality constraints

### Flood masks

MODIS and VIIRS offer frequent, consistent monitoring but are weak ground truth in equatorial wetlands. Optical products cannot see through cloud or dense canopy, 250–375 m pixels miss narrow or fragmented inundation, cloud shadows and wet soils can resemble water, and compositing can delay detection or miss peaks. These limitations are especially consequential during rainy-season rise and in small cultivated parcels.[^59][^60][^61][^62]

Permanent wetland, seasonal swamp and anomalous flood are classification choices rather than directly observed humanitarian categories. Copernicus comparisons in South Sudan found good broad agreement but a tendency to underestimate some flooded area and disagreements over permanent versus seasonal water. WFP’s thermal-amplitude method is useful because it is cloud-cleared and gap-filled, but it also embeds a spatially fixed threshold and classifies flood/wetland together.[^14][^63][^13]

A better workflow would use an ensemble of WFP thermal products, Sentinel-1 SAR, Copernicus GFM and optical detections; explicitly separate open water, inundated vegetation and low-confidence cloud gaps; and propagate disagreement into county exposure intervals.

### Population

WorldPop is valuable because it converts old county totals into a high-resolution surface, but the precision can be misleading. South Sudan’s last census was in 2008; older WorldPop surfaces redistribute those county totals using land-cover weights and national projections, so they do not directly observe post-independence settlement and displacement. Historical comparisons found county estimates differing by more than 20% between WorldPop and OCHA for 20 of 78 counties.[^64][^65]

The displacement-adjusted WorldPop/GRID3 product is preferable to an unadjusted surface, but its own documentation notes incomplete DTM payam coverage, potentially inaccurate GPS locations and omission of seasonal migration—precisely relevant during floods. Population exposure should therefore be reported as a range across WorldPop/GRID3, GHSL and settlement/building-constrained alternatives, with mobile IDP and returnee data added where available.[^66]

### Cropland and rangeland

ASAP’s 500 m cropland and rangeland layers are fractional masks and are static within a season rather than observations of planted area in the target year. They are suitable for broad screening, not field-level loss estimation in fragmented or shifting smallholder systems. African crop mapping is complicated by small plots, mixed crops, cloud cover and limited ground truth; combined optical and radar data generally perform better than a single coarse static mask.[^67][^68]

The distinction matters operationally. A pixel classified as cropland indicates potential agricultural land, not that it was planted, flooded during a sensitive crop stage, abandoned because of conflict or associated with a household that will lose food or income. FAO’s 2020 Jonglei assessment estimated planted area damaged, cereal loss and livestock effects using assessment evidence, demonstrating the extra information needed to move from exposure to impact.[^48]

FEWS livelihood zones add useful context, but they are categorical regional typologies rather than dynamic household vulnerability. A robust tool should combine them with current planting, market access, displacement, livestock movement and local coping capacity. It should avoid summing hectares, people and livestock into an arbitrary index without eliciting decision weights.

## What is genuinely useful

The proposal contains useful elements if their claims are narrowed:

- **Common operational view:** A reproducible pipeline can harmonize fragmented products into one county or, preferably, payam-level view for an NGO team. Integration and documentation may have practical value even when the component analyses are not novel.
- **Long-lead exposure scenarios:** There is still no mature, independently verified public product that gives probabilistic two-month county distributions for population, crop and livestock exposure across South Sudan. Extending aggregate INFLOW-AI output spatially, with honest uncertainty, could fill this gap.
- **Portfolio-conditioned ranking:** Ranking only counties where ZOA/Dorcas/DRA has mandate, partners, warehouse access and feasible transport would be more actionable than a national league table.
- **Marginal logistics decisions:** Forecast-informed adjustment of quantities, routes or timing within an existing prepositioning plan is a plausible use case.
- **Uncertainty and baseline testing:** Comparing a forecast with persistence and chronic-risk baselines would answer a genuinely unresolved scientific and operational question.
- **Reproducibility:** A versioned open method with archived inputs can reveal why different operational products disagree.

## What is duplicated

The following should not be presented as novel:

- Intersecting flood extent with gridded population and cropland at county level; WFP ADAM already does this.[^3]
- Coupling forecast hazard with population, cropland, grazing land, livestock and roads; Flood-PROOFS already implements this architecture.[^4]
- Producing a map of counties repeatedly exposed to flooding; REACH, World Bank and humanitarian plans already do so.[^21][^1][^2]
- Using antecedent inundation, upstream lake levels and rainfall to forecast Sudd extent at roughly two-month lead; INFLOW-AI and the Red Cross protocol now do this.[^5][^6]
- Ranking counties for humanitarian response without incorporating feasibility; OCHA, clusters and pooled funds already conduct richer intersectoral prioritization.[^8][^7]
- Advocating dry-season prepositioning in general; this has been standard practice for years.[^58][^57]

## Unsupported assumptions

### Unsupported or contradicted

1. **“NGOs primarily need a better national rank.”** Evaluations point more strongly to funding, access, infrastructure, storage, security and coordination constraints.[^24][^25][^23]
2. **“NGOs can simply switch counties annually.”** Public ZOA/Dorcas and DRA records show multi-year projects, offices, named partners and continuing locations.[^27][^26][^28]
3. **“Seasonal rainfall or May lake levels directly predict county flooding.”** The relationship is mediated by long storage and routing; WFP documents years when similar or lower lake levels preceded very different Sudd extents.[^13]
4. **“County-level two-to-three-month skill has been established.”** Aggregate Sudd forecasts are promising, but spatial county-impact verification is not yet demonstrated in the available publications.[^5][^6]
5. **“Satellite-exposed population or crops are observed impacts.”** They are modeled exposure estimates and inherit uncertainty from both flood and exposure datasets.[^43][^67][^66]
6. **“A six-to-sevenfold premium is universal.”** It is one plausible order of magnitude, but documented comparisons range widely.[^53][^54][^55][^56]
7. **“Prepositioning waits for a forecast.”** Agencies already preposition against recurrent seasonal access risk; forecasts would need to improve quantities or locations at the margin.[^18][^57]

### Still unresolved

- Whether a spatialized INFLOW-AI forecast beats last year’s map or long-run frequency at county or payam scale.
- Whether county ranking changes enough between years to influence NGO allocations.
- Whether early forecast information arrives before donor, procurement and transport decision deadlines.
- Whether the avoided costs for ZOA/DRA exceed the analytical, false-alarm, storage and reallocation costs.
- Whether local partners trust and use a probabilistic rank when it conflicts with field reports.

These unknowns should be treated as research questions, not premises.

## A better research contribution

### Recommended question

A stronger thesis would ask:

> **For ZOA/Dorcas or a DRA partner with a fixed operating footprint, does a two-month probabilistic flood-exposure forecast improve feasible prepositioning decisions over chronic-risk, persistence and current coordination baselines?**

This reframing is both more novel and more falsifiable. It permits the answer “no added value,” which is essential for an objective evaluation.

### Study design

#### 1. Start with the decision

Conduct a small number of structured interviews or decision workshops with country logistics, programme, security and MEAL staff. Document:

- The date by which procurement and transport decisions are actually locked.
- Existing hubs, partners and counties that can receive stock.
- Commodity shelf life, storage limits and sector mandate.
- Funding availability and donor restrictions.
- Minimum forecast confidence and tolerable false-alarm rate.
- Which decision can change: quantity, destination, route, timing or modality.

If no material decision remains open in May–June, the proposed tool has no operational value regardless of predictive skill.

#### 2. Define strong baselines

Evaluate at least:

- Fixed chronic-risk map based on long-run flood frequency.
- Previous-year flood extent or exposure.
- Current observed June inundation.
- OCHA/cluster priority counties.
- IPC or HNO rank alone.
- Routine NGO prepositioning plan.
- Aggregate INFLOW-AI or seasonal outlook without spatial exposure coupling.

The proposed model should earn its complexity by beating these baselines out of sample.

#### 3. Use probabilistic outputs

For each feasible county or payam, report an exposure distribution rather than one rank. Propagate uncertainty from:

- Alternative inundation products.
- Forecast ensembles and model parameters.
- Population surfaces and displacement updates.
- Cropland/rangeland masks.
- Administrative boundaries and missing observations.

Show probability of exceeding decision-relevant thresholds—for example, a specified population isolated or a route becoming unusable—rather than an opaque composite score.

#### 4. Validate in three layers

| Layer | Primary metric | Independent evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard | Spatial critical-success index, probability of detection, false-alarm ratio, area error and reliability | Independent sensor where possible; gauge or altimetry information; manually checked Sentinel-1 samples |
| Exposure/impact | Error and rank skill for displaced/affected people, damaged crop, livestock loss and service disruption | DTM, OCHA, FAO/CFSAM, REACH and facility assessments, with assessment-coverage flags |
| Decision | Cost-loss value, regret, stock-outs avoided, people reachable, tonnes shifted from air to surface and timeliness | Historical logistics records, warehouse data, invoices and reconstructed decisions |

Use blocked leave-one-year-out testing and a true prospective holdout. Do not randomly split pixels or dekads from the same flood season, because temporal autocorrelation would inflate skill.

#### 5. Model feasibility

Replace a national rank with a constrained allocation model. A simple formulation can maximize expected benefit subject to:

- Available budget and stock.
- Warehouse capacities.
- Surface transport lead times.
- Route closure probabilities.
- Partner coverage and sector mandate.
- Minimum service equity.
- Security and access constraints.

A transparent rule-based model may be preferable to a sophisticated optimizer if users cannot inspect or update the assumptions.

#### 6. Test value of information

Compare the expected loss under routine action with expected loss under forecast-conditioned action. Include false-positive costs such as unnecessary transport, storage loss and stock stranded in one hub, and false-negative costs such as late airlift and unmet need. Sensitivity analysis should identify the forecast skill, funding date and air-road cost ratio at which the forecast becomes worthwhile.

### Minimum viable scope

For a student project, national coverage is unnecessarily ambitious. A stronger scope would be:

- One operational footprint, such as DRA counties or one ZOA-Dorcas field-office network.
- Three to five retrospective flood seasons plus one prospective season.
- Population and road/access exposure as primary outcomes; cropland and livestock only where validation exists.
- A benchmark dashboard or notebook, not another permanent operational platform.
- A clear “stop” rule: if persistence performs equivalently or decisions cannot change at the forecast lead, recommend no deployment.

## Final assessment

The proposed approach addresses a real humanitarian problem, but its basic analytical recipe is no longer a strong novelty claim. County-level flood exposure overlays, seasonal risk narratives, chronic flood maps and coordinated priority lists already exist; a two-month Sudd extent forecast is now entering official anticipatory-action systems. A stand-alone national ranking risks becoming another dashboard alongside better-established products.[^1][^3][^6]

The most defensible contribution is therefore an **added-value and decision-value evaluation**, not an assumed product build. It should test whether spatialized seasonal forecasts outperform persistence for the NGO’s feasible footprint, whether the information arrives before real logistics deadlines, and whether it changes a controlled decision enough to offset false alarms and implementation costs. If those tests fail, the correct research finding is that static chronic-risk planning plus routine prepositioning and local operational judgment is preferable. If they succeed, the output should be integrated into existing Flood Taskforce, cluster and DRA workflows rather than launched as a parallel prioritization system.
