# Flood Anticipatory Action and Disaster Risk Financing in South Sudan

## Evidence verdict

The four assumptions are **only partly validated**. South Sudan operations strongly confirm fixed budgets, seasonal access collapse, dry-season pre-positioning and the value of staged action; however, published evidence does **not** support assumptions of near-total loss avoidance, a South Sudan flood-AA benefit-cost ratio, or a mature automatic two-trigger protocol already operating nationally.

| Assumption | Assessment | Empirical finding |
|---|---|---|
| Flood AA has high, observed BCRs | **Unproven for South Sudan floods** | Positive Horn of Africa ratios mainly concern drought and livestock; South Sudan flood allocations report outputs, not causal economic returns. |
| Early action avoids most losses | **Not supported** | Evaluations find partial protection of assets, health and coping capacity—not elimination of flood losses. |
| Access costs force earlier delivery | **Strongly supported** | Roads deteriorate rapidly after rains begin; wet-season air delivery has historically cost around six to seven times road or river alternatives. |
| Dykes are automatically no-regret | **Conditional** | Maintenance and reinforcement can be low-regret, but new or poorly engineered works can create substantial residual and downstream risk. |
| South Sudan uses formal two-stage triggers | **Partly supported** | Its 2023 response was operationally sequenced, but not documented as a formal forecast-based EAP with automatic readiness and activation thresholds. |

## Cost–loss evidence

A basic cost–loss rule activates when forecast probability \(p\) exceeds the ratio of action cost \(C\) to avoidable loss \(L\):

\[
p > \frac{C}{L}
\]

That is useful for designing a trigger, but \(L\) must represent the portion of loss the action can realistically prevent—not total expected disaster loss. IFRC guidance similarly describes matching lower-confidence forecasts to inexpensive, low-regret actions and reserving expensive actions for forecasts with higher likelihood.[web:735]

### Observed ratios

No publicly available evaluation identified in the reviewed evidence reports a **causally estimated BCR for flood AA in South Sudan**. South Sudan allocation documents principally report money allocated, people targeted, supplies delivered and infrastructure rehabilitated rather than monetized counterfactual losses.

The closest empirical Horn of Africa evidence is predominantly drought-related:

| Intervention | Location | Reported BCR | Important qualification |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Livestock feed and health support | Kenya, 2017 drought | 3.4–3.5 | Benefits included saved animals, extra milk and improved body condition.[web:671][web:674] |
| Same intervention with avoided food aid and restocking | Kenya | Up to 9 | The increase depends on including subsequent humanitarian costs that were assumed to be avoided.[web:676][web:680] |
| Livestock protection | Ethiopia | Approximately 6.9–7 | Preliminary calculation used historical mortality baselines and production assumptions rather than a randomized counterfactual.[web:671][web:680] |
| Desert-locust control | Greater Horn | Approximately 15 | This was large-scale preventive pest control, not flood AA; losses were modelled from protected crop, pasture and milk production.[web:673][web:678] |

These are **field-informed ex-post economic calculations**, but not pure observations of the difference between actual treated and untreated disaster losses. The Kenya calculation, for example, combined observed herd condition and milk outcomes with valuations and inferred avoided mortality; the Ethiopia estimate relied explicitly on historical mortality and production baselines.[web:671]

### Loss-avoidance percentage

A BCR cannot be translated directly into a percentage of total disaster damage avoided. Its numerator may combine:

- Assets preserved.
- Additional production.
- Improved market value.
- Avoided borrowing or distress sales.
- Avoided future relief and restocking.
- Health and welfare benefits.

A rigorous Bangladesh flood evaluation found anticipatory cash improved evacuation, health, well-being, livestock and asset protection, and reduced high-interest borrowing and distress sales. It found no detectable effect on some food-based coping behaviours or on faster restoration of productive capacity, demonstrating material but incomplete mitigation.[web:683]

A later peer-reviewed assessment characterized post-flood effect sizes as broad but modest relative to a flood lasting more than a month and a transfer worth approximately two weeks of household food expenditure.[web:685] Therefore, neither 100% nor a general figure above 50% is empirically defensible; the avoided fraction is action-, asset-, timing- and hazard-specific.

The broader evidence base remains weak: reviews find few independent or peer-reviewed studies, incompatible definitions of benefits and costs, and substantial reliance on implementing agencies’ own monitoring.[web:682][web:684]

## False alarms

### Economic treatment

Forecast-based financing accepts that some activations will occur “in vain.” The economic test is whether expected avoided harm across many activations exceeds action costs, including false-alarm costs:

\[
\text{Expected net benefit} = pB-C-R
\]

Here \(B\) is the benefit if the damaging event occurs and the action succeeds, while \(R\) represents residual costs such as disruption, opportunity cost, mistrust or inappropriate assistance.

Agencies reduce \(R\) by:

- Choosing actions useful under several plausible scenarios.
- Separating readiness expenditure from final distributions.
- Updating forecasts between stages.
- Limiting irreversible action until confidence rises.
- Retaining or returning unused activation funds.
- Reviewing triggers after false positives and missed events.

### Operational experience

CERF’s 2021 Nepal flood pilot provides a concrete false-alarm case. Its readiness threshold released funds and initiated preparation, but the later action threshold was not reached; most funds were ultimately returned, and the evaluation concluded that a single threshold and monitoring station generated poor basin-wide activation behaviour.[web:121]

False alarms are not politically neutral. Studies document government and donor reluctance to spend on uncertain forecasts, concern over loss of control, possible hostile media reaction, and low visibility of disasters that were successfully mitigated.[web:691] Repeated false alarms can also erode trust, while model reviews warn that false positives still misallocate scarce resources even when AA is described as “no regret.”[web:306][web:688]

Nevertheless, no reviewed document establishes a universal rule that a false-alarm rate above 50% automatically causes defunding. The consequences depend on:

- Cost per activation.
- Whether the actions retain value.
- Transparency around forecast uncertainty.
- Missed-event rates.
- Funding scarcity.
- Public and political communication.
- Whether evaluation covers many seasons rather than one activation.

## Budget rationing

South Sudan AA does **not** operate through an open-ended entitlement under which every county crossing a threshold automatically receives full assistance. Funding is allocated through finite envelopes and then geographically, sectorally and institutionally prioritized.

Examples include:

- The 2021 South Sudan Humanitarian Fund allocation had a ceiling of $20 million for flooding, conflict and disease response, with approximately $9.6 million assigned to flood response across selected counties.
- The 2023 FAO flood early-action project had a fixed $1 million budget for Rubkona and Malakal, targeting specified numbers of households, animals and livelihood kits.[web:699]
- In April 2023, SSHF allocated $11.5 million for dykes, roads and pre-positioning in Unity; in May it added $11.9 million for Unity and Upper Nile, including supplies, seeds, disease surveillance and WASH rehabilitation.[web:723]

In practice, rationing uses combinations of:

- Forecast flood severity.
- IPC food-security phase.
- Displacement and returnee concentrations.
- Previous flood impacts and residual water.
- Access and partner presence.
- Uncovered sectoral gaps.
- Complementarity with CERF and other donors.
- Partner capacity to procure, pre-finance and deliver rapidly.

The FAO project also illustrates implementation friction: it was planned to begin in June 2023 but recorded an actual start in September. Its budget included air transport, warehousing and last-mile transport, showing how delays can move an nominally anticipatory project into the expensive response period.[web:699]

DRA financing has comparable constraints. Its institutional evaluation found that rapid NGO-level proposal development could be offset by grant-approval delays, causing higher operational costs and missed implementation windows; it also warned that inclusive funding processes can fragment fixed budgets into small, inefficient projects.[web:726] The current South Sudan Joint Response likewise specifies finite beneficiary numbers across eight locations rather than an unlimited threshold-based commitment.[web:727]

## Seasonal logistics

### Access timeline

| Period | Typical operational condition | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| January–April | Dry-season road window | Maximum use of heavy trucking, road repair and bulk pre-positioning. |
| May–June | Rains begin; conditions deteriorate unevenly | Last feasible window for some heavy works and truck convoys; uncertainty remains over eventual flood magnitude. |
| July–October | Peak road disruption and inundation | Many deep-field locations require air, river, boat or highly specialized vehicles. |
| November–December | Water may recede, but residual flooding persists | Road recovery varies substantially; Unity, Jonglei and Upper Nile can remain isolated. |

A June 2023 Logistics Cluster update reported that roads were still broadly passable but deteriorating rapidly, with conditions expected to worsen within four to five weeks.[web:706] WFP has estimated that around 60% of South Sudan’s unpaved roads can disappear for roughly six months as black-cotton soil turns to mud.[web:708]

Engineering guidance explains the mechanism: expansive black-cotton clay absorbs large quantities of water and undergoes an almost total loss of strength. Effective stabilization is possible but expensive in South Sudan’s low-volume-road context.[web:713]

### Transport premiums

The strongest public evidence concerns relative rather than current county-specific tariffs:

- WFP reported that once roads close, delivering food by air costs approximately seven times road transport.[web:703]
- A South Sudan logistics example put specialized ground delivery at about $450 per metric tonne against approximately $2,400 for helicopter airdrops, a ratio of roughly 1:5.3.[web:712]
- UN reporting described airdrops as around six times more expensive than river or road transport.[web:714]
- SSHF reported that pre-positioning by road created cost efficiencies by avoiding expensive airlifting; WFP’s national strategy explicitly seeks to replace air movements with road and river transport and to pre-position during the dry season.[web:715]

These figures should not be treated as current quotations for a particular Unity, Jonglei or Upper Nile corridor. Rates depend on fuel prices, security escorts, backhaul availability, aircraft type, airstrip condition, cargo density and last-mile distance; no comparable public 2023–2026 lane-level tariff table was identified.

River transport can carry large volumes more cheaply than air, particularly into Upper Nile. It is slower, schedule-dependent and exposed to insecurity, port limitations and last-mile delivery constraints; a WFP convoy to Ulang took approximately one week but replaced much more costly air delivery.[web:712]

### Timing implication

The logistics evidence strongly supports starting **physical** AA during May–June—or earlier—even when seasonal flood forecasts remain uncertain. Waiting for higher confidence in July or August can eliminate the road option and convert an affordable trucking operation into an air-dependent one.

South Sudan’s 2023 sequencing reflects this logic: April and May allocations supported dykes, access-road rehabilitation, supply pre-positioning, agricultural inputs and WASH rehabilitation before peak rains.[web:723] Humanitarian planning documents continue to call for maximizing dry-season road pre-positioning because it is the least expensive transport mode.[web:720]

However, longer lead time should not mean committing every intervention in May. The operational solution is to commit early only to actions whose benefits survive a forecast error.

## Regret classification

“No-regret” or “low-regret” actions are officially defined as measures that benefit the population irrespective of whether the predicted disaster occurs. Some definitions also require benefits to exceed the opportunity cost of using the money elsewhere.[web:738]

Consequently, the category belongs to the **specific design and context**, not automatically to an intervention label.

| Intervention | Likely classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Reinforcing an existing communal dyke | Usually low-regret | Extends protection of settlements and infrastructure under ordinary and extreme floods, provided the dyke is technically sound and maintained. |
| Clearing blocked drainage | Usually low-regret | Reduces routine standing water, disease risk and localized waterlogging even without an extreme flood. |
| Protecting or raising borehole heads | Low-regret | Preserves water quality and service continuity under recurrent flooding. |
| Elevating reusable storage | Low-regret | Retains value over multiple seasons and protects several categories of supplies. |
| Rehabilitating an access road | Low-regret where recurrently used | Improves humanitarian, market and civilian mobility outside the target flood season. |
| Constructing a new major dyke | Potentially high-regret | High sunk cost, uncertain hydraulic effects, maintenance obligations and possible transfer of floodwater downstream. |
| Unconditional anticipatory cash | Mixed | Rarely physically wasted, but may have substantial targeting and opportunity costs if issued widely after false alarms. |
| Short-shelf-life seed distribution | Medium to high regret | Value depends on correct variety, planting window, land access and subsequent rainfall or recession conditions. |
| Livestock vaccination | Often low-regret | Can reduce endemic and flood-associated disease beyond the immediate forecast, assuming appropriate disease targeting and cold-chain delivery. |
| Evacuation | High-regret unless risk is imminent | False activation may interrupt livelihoods, expose people to protection risks and weaken future compliance. |

IFRC lists reinforcement of homes and infrastructure alongside evacuation, cash and protection kits as possible anticipatory actions, but its broad description of AA as “no-regrets” should not be interpreted as proof that every listed action has zero downside.[web:731][web:737]

South Sudan provides credible low-regret examples. The 2023 allocations reinforced dykes around displaced communities and infrastructure, rehabilitated access roads and WASH systems, and pre-positioned supplies.[web:723] ZOA/Dorcas also reports a dyke project intended to prevent flooding, reclaim farmland and allow displaced families to resettle, although the public description does not provide an independent BCR or counterfactual impact evaluation.[web:729]

## Two-stage triggers

### Formal model

The operationally sensible architecture is:

1. **Readiness trigger:** A lower-confidence, long-lead forecast releases a limited readiness budget for road repair, dyke inspection, drainage clearing, procurement, contracting, staff deployment and pre-positioning.
2. **Activation trigger:** A higher-confidence, short-lead forecast or observed river condition releases cash, evacuation support and geographically targeted distributions.
3. **Impact confirmation:** Observations determine whether response or recovery funding is needed and where.

IFRC’s forecast-based financing model explicitly supports matching low-probability forecasts with low-regret actions and high-probability forecasts with more expensive actions.[web:735] WFP similarly describes phased anticipatory action progressing from low-cost, no-regret measures to resource-intensive operations.[web:112]

### Documented example

The CERF Nepal flood framework used distinct readiness and action thresholds. The readiness trigger allowed funding and preparation before the later action decision, and the 2021 false alarm demonstrated that this separation can prevent full distribution when updated evidence no longer supports it.[web:121]

### South Sudan status

South Sudan’s 2023 flood operation was **multi-stage in practice**:

- April: dykes, roads and pre-positioning in Unity.
- May: additional readiness and livelihood measures in Unity and Upper Nile.
- September: CERF financing for current flooding and preparation for further flooding.[web:723]

This was sequenced risk financing, but the reviewed public documents do not show a formal EAP specifying a May forecast probability as Stage 1 and a July or August river-level threshold as Stage 2. Decisions appear to have combined forecasts, existing inundation, food insecurity, displacement, access and humanitarian judgement.

South Sudan is still strengthening the institutional prerequisites for a national trigger system. Regional documentation identifies the need for more skilful national forecasts, stronger meteorological capacity, harmonized thresholds and subnational technical structures.[web:64]

Start Network reports a multi-agency, multi-year financing approach that can be layered with CERF and IFRC mechanisms, but available public documentation does not establish a South Sudan flood protocol with two automatic stages.[web:64] Likewise, DRA’s public South Sudan materials describe multisector resilience and response programming, but not a forecast-calibrated readiness/activation EAP.[web:727][web:728]

## Operational implications

For Unity, Jonglei and Upper Nile, the evidence supports a **tiered fixed-envelope model**, not open-ended activation:

- Fund baseline dyke, drainage, borehole and road maintenance before the rainy season.
- Use an early forecast trigger to release capped logistics and pre-positioning funds in April–June.
- Rank counties using expected human impact, access-loss probability, displacement, existing inundation and uncovered needs.
- Reserve late-stage funds for cash, evacuation and consumables once spatial confidence improves.
- Evaluate each action against the loss category it can plausibly reduce rather than total disaster losses.
- Report false alarms, misses, avoided air-transport expenditure, residual losses and durable asset value separately.
- Avoid presenting BCRs from Horn of Africa drought-livestock interventions as empirical evidence for South Sudan flood protection.

The most defensible empirical conclusion is therefore not that AA prevents most flood damage. It is that **early logistics and durable protection can reduce selected losses and avoid very large wet-season delivery premiums**, while consumable or disruptive interventions require later and more precise activation.
